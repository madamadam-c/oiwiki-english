We restrict the geometric problems to be solved to the two-dimensional plane, which involves the basic knowledge of two-dimensional computational geometry. When studying such problems, figures are usually placed in a Cartesian coordinate system or a polar coordinate system, which makes solving them much more convenient.

## Representing Figures

### Points

In a Cartesian coordinate system, a point is represented by its x- and y-coordinates, such as point $(5,2)$ or point $(-1,0)$. Since coordinates are ordered pairs, they can be stored using [`std::pair`](https://en.cppreference.com/w/cpp/utility/pair.html), or by defining a structure.

In a polar coordinate system, a point is represented by its radius and polar angle, and can be stored similarly to the Cartesian-coordinate representation.

### Vectors

Since the coordinate representation of a vector is the same as that of a point, store a vector just like a point.

The same applies in a polar coordinate system.

### Lines

Representing lines in a polar coordinate system is relatively difficult, so we only discuss the Cartesian-coordinate case.

#### Lines and Rays

When solving mathematical problems, we generally represent a line with an analytic expression. A line can be written in many forms, such as the general form $Ax+By+C=0$, slope-intercept form $y=kx+b$, and intercept form $\dfrac{x}{a}+\dfrac{y}{b}=1$, but these forms ultimately serve analytic equation solving. In some computational geometry problems, using analytic expressions for lines increases the difficulty of solving the problem.

In computational geometry, a line is usually represented by a point on the line and the line's direction vector, while a ray is represented by its endpoint and direction vector. A direction vector is a nonzero vector parallel to the line, usually taken as a unit vector. There are two unit vectors parallel to a line, and either may be chosen depending on the problem; for a ray, the direction vector is usually chosen to point in the same direction as the ray extends.

#### Segments

To represent a segment, it is enough to store its two endpoints.

### Polygons

Use an array to record every vertex of the polygon in a certain order. For a simple polygon, vertices are usually recorded in clockwise or counterclockwise order.

In particular, when representing a rectangle whose sides are all parallel to the coordinate axes, it is enough to record the two vertices on one diagonal.

### Curved Figures

For some special curves, such as function graphs, usually record their analytic expressions. For a circle, simply record its center and radius.

## Basic Formulas

### Sine Rule

In $\triangle \text{ABC}$, if the sides opposite angles $A,B,C$ are $a,b,c$ respectively, then:

$$
\frac{a}{\sin A}=\frac{b}{\sin B}=\frac{c}{\sin C}=2R
$$

where $R$ is the circumradius of $\triangle \text{ABC}$.

### Cosine Rule

In $\triangle \text{ABC}$, if the sides opposite angles $A,B,C$ are $a,b,c$ respectively, then:

$$
\begin{aligned}
a^2&=b^2+c^2-2bc\cos A\\
b^2&=a^2+c^2-2ac\cos B\\
c^2&=a^2+b^2-2ab\cos C
\end{aligned}
$$

The proofs of the formulas above are omitted. They are all standard high-school mathematics content.

## Basic Operations

### Determining Which Side of a Line a Point Lies On

Suppose we have a point $P$ on a line and the line's direction vector $\mathbf v$, and want to know which side of the line a point $Q$ lies on. The phrase "which side" here is relative to the direction vector; that is, we judge while facing the direction pointed to by the direction vector.

Using the properties of the vector product, compute $\mathbf v\times \overrightarrow {PQ}$. By the right-hand rule, if the cross product is negative, then $Q$ lies to the right of the line; if it is $0$, then $Q$ lies on the line; if it is positive, then $Q$ lies to the left of the line.

### Bounding-Box Test and Straddling Test

Now we want to determine whether two segments intersect.

First handle some special cases. If two segments are parallel, naturally they cannot intersect, unless they are collinear. This case can be checked by determining whether the slopes of the lines containing the segments are equal.

Of course, if two segments coincide or partially overlap, or if their intersection is an endpoint of one segment, it is enough to check whether three points are collinear.

There are also cases that obviously do not intersect, informally described as "the two segments are too far apart". Define the "region of a segment" as the rectangle using this segment as a diagonal, with all sides parallel to coordinate axes. Then if two segments have no common region, they definitely do not intersect.

Thus we can quickly determine that the two segments do not intersect.

This is the **bounding-box test**. The case above is called **failing the bounding-box test**.

Failing the bounding-box test is a **sufficient but not necessary condition** for two segments to have no intersection, so further checks are still needed.

If two segments $a,b$ intersect, the two endpoints of segment $b$ must lie on opposite sides of the line containing segment $a$; similarly, the two endpoints of segment $a$ must lie on opposite sides of the line containing segment $b$. We can directly determine the positions of one segment's endpoints relative to the line containing the other segment. If they differ, the two segments intersect; otherwise they do not. As mentioned in the previous section, the relation between a line and a point can be determined using the cross product.

This is the **straddling test**. If, for two segments $a,b$, the two endpoints of segment $b$ lie on opposite sides of the line containing segment $a$, **and**  the two endpoints of segment $a$ lie on opposite sides of the line containing segment $b$, then we say that segments $a,b$ **pass the straddling test**, meaning the two segments intersect.

Note that when two segments are collinear but do not intersect, they may also pass the straddling test, so accurate determination requires combining it with the bounding-box test.

### Determining Whether a Point Lies Inside a Polygon

In computational geometry, this problem is called the [PIP problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point_in_polygon). There are several mature methods, introduced below in order.

#### Ray Casting Algorithm

The original idea can be found [here](https://wrfranklin.org/Research/Short_Notes/pnpoly.html).

First handle some special cases, such as "the point is too far away from the polygon". Similar to judging segment intersection, consider the smallest rectangle that can completely cover the polygon. If the point is not inside this rectangle, then it is definitely not inside the polygon. Such a rectangle is easy to find: just know the minimum and maximum x- and y-coordinates of the polygon, and combine them pairwise to get the four vertices of the rectangle.

The cases where the point lies on an edge or vertex of the polygon are also easy to determine. For each edge, check whether the point lies on the edge or on a vertex.

Consider drawing a ray starting from this point. If the ray has an odd number of intersections with the polygon, the point lies inside the polygon; otherwise it lies outside. This is often summarized as **odd means inside, even means outside**. The algorithm is also called the even-odd rule.

By the [Jordan curve theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jordan_curve_theorem), every time this ray intersects an edge of the polygon, it switches between being inside and outside the polygon. Therefore, it is enough to count the parity of the number of intersections.

For the chosen ray, the slope of the line containing it can be chosen randomly. It is recommended to use an irrational number to avoid the case where the ray coincides with an edge of the polygon.

In the original code, the last point in the array recording the polygon is used as a point on the ray. Then, when counting, if the ray passes through an edge or vertex of the polygon, the points that the ray passes through can be stipulated to be on the same side of the ray, after which the straddling test can be applied.

???+ warning "Note"
    The ray casting algorithm only applies to simple polygons. For polygons with self-intersecting edges, the Jordan curve theorem does not apply, so this method cannot determine the result.

#### Winding Number Algorithm

The winding number is a mathematical concept: the total number of times a closed curve winds counterclockwise around the point in the plane. It is easy to see that when the winding number is $0$, the point lies outside the curve. This algorithm is also called the nonzero rule.

How do we compute it? Connect the point to all vertices of the polygon and compute the sum of the angles between adjacent segments. Note that these angles are **oriented**. If the angle sum is $0$, the point lies outside the polygon; otherwise it lies inside.

### Finding the Intersection of Two Lines

First, we need to determine whether two lines intersect. It is enough to check whether their direction vectors are parallel. If the direction vectors are parallel, the two lines are parallel and have $0$ intersections. Furthermore, if two parallel lines pass through the same point, then the two lines coincide.

Thus the problem is simplified to: given lines $AB,CD$ that intersect at one point, find the intersection point $E$.

If two lines intersect, they have only one intersection point. Since we recorded a point on the line and the line's direction vector, we only need to know the distance $l$ between that point and the intersection point, then translate the point by $l$ units along the direction vector.

Construct a triangle and use the sine rule to solve for $l$; the vector product can be used to build the sine-rule relation.

![Intersection](./images/2d-intersection.svg)

From the figure above, $|\mathbf a\times \mathbf b|=|\mathbf a||\mathbf b|\sin \beta$, and $|\mathbf u\times \mathbf b|=|\mathbf u||\mathbf b|\sin \theta$.

Taking the quotient gives:

$$
T=\frac{|\mathbf u\times \mathbf b|}{|\mathbf a\times \mathbf b|}=\frac{|\mathbf u|\sin \theta}{|\mathbf a|\sin \beta}
$$

We can see that $\left|\dfrac{|\mathbf u|\sin \theta}{\sin \beta}\right|=l$. If the expression inside the absolute value is positive, translate along direction $\mathbf a$; otherwise translate in the opposite direction.

At the same time, directly multiplying $T$ by $\mathbf a$ automatically introduces the unit vector of the line, so no other cancellation is needed.

Therefore, simply subtract $T\mathbf a$ from point $B$ to obtain the intersection point.

### Finding the Perimeter and Area of Any Polygon

#### Finding the Perimeter of Any Polygon

Compute it directly; simplicity is a virtue.

#### Finding the Area of Any Polygon

Using the geometric meaning of the magnitude of the vector product, we can compute it with cross products.

Label the points of the polygon counterclockwise as $p_1,p_2,\cdots ,p_n$, choose any auxiliary point $O$, and let $\mathbf v_i=p_i-O$. Then the area $S$ of the polygon can be expressed as:

$$
S=\frac{1}{2}\left|\sum_{i=1}^n \mathbf v_i\times \mathbf v_{(i\bmod n)+1}\right|
$$

### Circles and Lines

#### Finding the Intersections of a Line and a Circle

First determine the positional relationship between the line and the circle. If the line and circle are disjoint, there is no intersection. If they are tangent, use the tangent line to find the line containing the tangency point and the radius, then transform the problem into finding the intersection of two lines.

If there are two intersections, use the Pythagorean theorem to find the midpoint of the two intersection points, then add the half-chord length along the line direction.

#### Finding the Intersections of Two Circles

First determine the positional relationship between the two circles. If they are externally disjoint or one contains the other, there is no intersection. If they are tangent, compute the direction vector of the line connecting the two centers, then use the two radii to compute the translation distance, and finally translate the center along this direction vector.

If the two circles intersect, there must be two intersection points, symmetric about the line connecting the centers. Therefore, only the method for finding one intersection is described below; the other can be found similarly.

First connect the center of one circle to the intersection point, and find the angle between the line connecting the centers and this segment. Then rotating the direction vector of the line connecting the centers by this angle gives the direction vector of the radius from the center to the intersection point. Translate the center by the radius length along this direction vector to obtain one intersection point.

## Polar-Angle Order

Generally, this type of problem requires first enumerating a pole, then computing the polar coordinates of the other points, and solving the problem in polar-angle order under the polar coordinate system.

???+ example "[JOISC 2014 Day4, Two People's Constellations](https://loj.ac/p/2882)"
    There are $n$ points in the plane and three colors; each point has one of the three colors. Find the number of pairs of disjoint tricolored triangles. $6\le n\le 3000$.

??? note "Solution"
    If two triangles do not intersect, then two internal common tangents can be drawn. If they intersect or one contains the other, internal common tangents cannot be drawn. Common tangents of triangles can be treated analogously to common tangents of circles.
    
    First enumerate an origin, denoted $O$. Use this point as the pole, and use the line through this point parallel to the $x$-axis as the polar axis to establish a polar coordinate system. Sort the remaining points by increasing polar angle. Then count the number of points of each color above and below the polar axis.
    
    Then enumerate common tangents by point. Let the currently enumerated point be $P$; initially the common tangent is the polar axis. Start counting. There must be a common tangent passing through points $O$ and $P$. Since the common tangent does not intersect the triangles, if one triangle chooses points above the tangent, the other must choose points below it. Then use the multiplication principle to count the number of schemes.
    
    After counting, rotate the common tangent. Point $P$ must change its position relative to the common tangent from above to below or vice versa, while other points do not move, so only its position information should be changed. In this way, the same pair of triangles is eventually counted $4$ times, because the same common tangent is enumerated twice. Therefore, the final answer should be divided by $4$.
    
    Analyze the algorithm complexity. We enumerate an origin, then for each origin sort the remaining points and count linearly. Therefore, the time complexity is $O(n^2\log n)$.

## Implementation Notes

Computational geometry often performs floating-point calculations of type `double`, which introduces precision issues and time-cost issues.

For some problems, such as finding the area of a triangle whose point coordinates are all integers, we can use their special properties to perform purely integer computations and avoid floating-point precision effects.

Because reading and computing floating-point numbers are both slower than for integers, pay attention to the effect of constant factors on running time.
