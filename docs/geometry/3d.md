Many concepts in three-dimensional geometry are analogous to those in [two-dimensional geometry](./2d.md), and we can solve three-dimensional geometry problems with methods similar to those used for two-dimensional geometry.

## Basic Concepts

Concepts such as points, vectors, and lines are similar to those in two-dimensional geometry, so they are not expanded here.

### Plane

We can represent a plane using a point $P_0(x_0,y_0,z_0)$ on the plane and the plane's normal vector, that is, a vector perpendicular to the plane, $\boldsymbol{n}$.

Because $\boldsymbol{n}$ is perpendicular to the plane, $\boldsymbol{n}$ is perpendicular to every line in that plane. In other words, if $\boldsymbol{n}=(A,B,C)$, then every point $P(x,y,z)$ on the plane satisfies $\boldsymbol{n} \cdot \overrightarrow{PP_0} = 0$.

By the definition of the vector dot product, the expression above is equivalent to:

$$
A(x-x_0)+B(y-y_0)+C(z-z_0)=0
$$

After rearranging, we get:

$$
Ax+By+Cz-(Ax_0+By_0+Cz_0)=0
$$

Let $D=-(Ax_0+By_0+Cz_0)$. Then the expression becomes $Ax+By+Cz+D=0$. We call this the **general form** of a plane.

## Basic Operations

### Angles Between Lines and Planes

Using spatial vectors, angles between lines and planes in space can be found quickly.

For two skew lines $a$ and $b$, choose a point $P$ in space and draw $a' \parallel a$ and $b' \parallel b'$. The acute or right angle formed by $a'$ and $b'$ is called the **angle between the two skew lines** $a$ and $b$.

For a line $a$ and a plane $\alpha$, if $a$ intersects $\alpha$ at $A$, draw a perpendicular from a point $P$ on $a$ to plane $\alpha$ and let it meet $\alpha$ at $O$. The complement of the angle between $a$ and $PO$ is called the **angle between the line and the plane**. In particular, if $a \parallel \alpha$ or $a \subset \alpha$, the angle between them is $0^\circ$.

For two planes $\alpha$ and $\beta$, their angle is defined as the angle formed by two lines $a,b$ perpendicular to their intersection line $l$, where $a \subset \alpha$ and $b \subset \beta$.

#### Definition of the Angle Between Two Lines and Necessary and Sufficient Conditions

-   The angle between the direction vectors of two lines is called the angle between the two lines.

From this proposition, we obtain the following conclusion: given two lines $l_1, l_2$ with direction vectors $s_1 (m_1, n_1, p_1)$ and $s_2 (m_2, n_2, p_2)$ respectively, and letting $\varphi$ be the angle between the two lines, we get $\cos \varphi = \dfrac{\left | m_1m_2+n_1n_2+p_1p_2 \right |}{\sqrt{m_1^2+n_1^2+p_1^2}\sqrt{m_2^2+n_2^2+p_2^2}}$.

-   $l_1 \perp l_2 \iff m_1m_2 + n_1n_2 + p_1p_2 = 0$

-   $l_1 \parallel l_2 \iff \dfrac{m_1}{m_2} = \dfrac{n_1}{n_2} = \dfrac{p_1}{p_2}$.

### Angle Between a Three-Dimensional Vector and a Plane

When a line is not perpendicular to a plane, the angle $\varphi$ ($\varphi \in [0, \frac{\pi}{2}]$) between the line and its projection onto the plane is called the angle between the line and the plane.

Let the line vector be $s(m, n, p)$ and the plane's normal vector be $f(a, b, c)$. Then the following propositions hold:

-   Sine of the angle: $\sin\varphi = \dfrac{\left | am + bn + cp \right |}{\sqrt{a^2+b^2+c^2}\sqrt{m^2+n^2+p^2}}$

-   The line is parallel to the plane $\iff am+bn+cp = 0$

-   The line is perpendicular to the plane $\iff \dfrac{a}{m} = \dfrac{b}{n} = \dfrac{c}{p}$

## Solid Geometry Theorems

### Three-Sine Theorem

Let the measure of dihedral angle $M－AB－N$ be $\alpha$. On plane $M$, there is a ray $AC$ that forms angle $\beta$ with edge $AB$ and angle $\gamma$ with plane $N$. Then $\sin\gamma = \sin\alpha\cdot\sin\beta$.

### Three-Cosine Theorem

Let $O$ be a point on a plane. The projection of line $BO$ through a point $B$ outside the plane onto the plane is $AO$, and $OC$ is a line on the plane. Then the cosine relationship among $\angle COB，\angle AOC，\angle AOB$ is $\cos\angle BOC=\cos\angle AOB\cdot\cos\angle AOC$ ($\angle AOC$，$\angle AOB$ can only be acute angles).
