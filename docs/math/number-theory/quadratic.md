This article provides a brief introduction to the concepts related to quadratic fields. Two important examples are Gaussian integers and Eisenstein integers, which can be used to solve some number theory problems.

## Basic Concepts

This section first introduces a series of basic concepts. Quadratic fields and quadratic integer rings are both special cases of the more general concepts of algebraic extension fields and algebraic integer rings, so almost all definitions and conclusions in this section can be appropriately generalized to the general case. The discussion in this section will be limited to the special case of quadratic fields without introducing too much algebraic number theory content.

### Quadratic Fields

Elements of quadratic fields are all quadratic algebraic numbers.

A **quadratic algebraic number** is a complex number that can be expressed as a solution of a univariate quadratic equation with integer coefficients. By the quadratic formula, all quadratic algebraic numbers can be expressed in the form

$$
a + b\sqrt{d}
$$

where $a$ and $b$ are rational numbers, and $d$ is a squarefree integer. Any number of this form is also a quadratic algebraic number. Quadratic algebraic numbers can be divided into rational numbers and **quadratic irrational numbers**. Clearly, a quadratic irrational number has a unique representation in the above form.

For any squarefree integer $d \neq 0, 1$, one can verify that the set $Q(\sqrt{d}) = \{a + b\sqrt{d} : a, b \in \mathbf{Q}\}$ is closed under addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division. Since a set closed under the four arithmetic operations is called a [field](../algebra/basic.md#field), the set $Q(\sqrt{d})$ is called a **quadratic field**. Every quadratic field contains all rational numbers, so they are all [quadratic extensions](../algebra/field-theory.md#field-extensions) of the rational number field $\mathbf{Q}$. When $d > 0$, all numbers in the quadratic field $\mathbf{Q}(\sqrt{d})$ are real numbers, and $\mathbf{Q}(\sqrt{d})$ is called a real quadratic field; when $d < 0$, all numbers in the quadratic field except rational numbers are complex numbers, and $\mathbf{Q}(\sqrt{d})$ is called an imaginary quadratic field.

### Conjugate and Norm

The **conjugate** of the quadratic irrational number $a + b\sqrt{d}$ is defined as $a - b\sqrt{d}$. Conjugate quadratic irrational numbers are the two distinct roots of the same integer-coefficient quadratic equation. In real quadratic fields, the conjugate of a quadratic irrational number does not coincide with its complex conjugate; in imaginary quadratic fields, the conjugate of a quadratic irrational number is its complex conjugate. Finally, the conjugate of a rational number is defined as itself. This defines the concept of conjugate for all quadratic algebraic numbers.

In any quadratic field, equations generated by the four arithmetic operations of addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division cannot distinguish between conjugates. That is, in any equation, replacing each number with its conjugate, or equivalently changing the sign inside each square root, the equation remains true.

Using conjugates, several mappings from quadratic algebraic numbers to rational numbers can be constructed, which can transform discussions about quadratic algebraic numbers into discussions about rational numbers. The simpler one is the **trace** of a quadratic algebraic number $\alpha$, which is the sum of a number and its conjugate, denoted $\operatorname{tr}(\alpha)$. Since this is actually twice its rational part, it does not provide much information.

More useful is the norm of a quadratic algebraic number. The product of a quadratic algebraic number and its conjugate is called its **norm**:

$$
N(a + b\sqrt{d}) = a^2 - db^2
$$

Obviously, in an imaginary quadratic field, the concept of norm coincides with the concept of the square of the absolute value of a complex number; but in a real quadratic field, these two concepts do not coincide.

The norm has good properties. First, since $d$ is squarefree and cannot be a perfect square, only $0$ has norm $0$. Second, the norm preserves multiplication and division:

$$
N(a_1 + b_1\sqrt{d}) N(a_2 + b_2\sqrt{d}) = N((a_1 + b_1\sqrt{d})(a_2 + b_2\sqrt{d}))
$$

$$
\frac{N(a_1 + b_1\sqrt{d})}{N(a_2 + b_2\sqrt{d})} = N\left(\frac{a_1 + b_1\sqrt{d}}{a_2 + b_2\sqrt{d}}\right)
$$

Additionally, the reciprocal of a quadratic algebraic number is the ratio of its conjugate to its norm:

$$
\frac{1}{a + b\sqrt{d}} = \frac{a - b\sqrt{d}}{N(a + b\sqrt{d})}
$$

Using Vieta's theorem, the quadratic algebraic number $\alpha$ is actually a root of the equation

$$
x^2 - \operatorname{tr}(\alpha)x + N(\alpha) = 0
$$

The discriminant of this equation is also called the **discriminant** of the quadratic algebraic number, denoted $\operatorname{disc}(\alpha)$, with value $4db^2$.

???+ note "Matrix representation"
    Similar to complex numbers, quadratic algebraic numbers can also be represented by matrices. Let $d \neq 0,1$ be a squarefree integer and $a, b$ be rational numbers, then $a + b\sqrt{d}$ can be represented by the matrix

    $$
    \begin{pmatrix} a & b \\ db & a \end{pmatrix}
    $$

    It can be verified that the results of addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division of this matrix correspond to the operations on quadratic algebraic numbers. The trace and determinant of this matrix correspond to the trace and norm of the quadratic algebraic number, and the discriminant of the characteristic polynomial of the matrix is the discriminant of the quadratic algebraic number. The adjugate matrix of this matrix corresponds to the conjugate of the quadratic algebraic number.

### Quadratic Integer Rings

Among quadratic algebraic numbers, the more special ones are quadratic integers. A **quadratic integer** is a complex number that can be expressed as a solution of a univariate quadratic equation with leading coefficient one and integer coefficients. The only difference from quadratic algebraic numbers is the restriction on the leading coefficient. By the quadratic formula, the two roots of the quadratic equation $x^2 + px + q = 0$ are

$$
\frac{-p \pm \sqrt{p^2 - 4q}}{2}
$$

If $p$ is even, i.e., $p = 2k$, then the two roots can be written as $-k \pm \sqrt{k^2 - q}$; if $p$ is odd, i.e., $p = 2k + 1$, then the two roots can be written as $-k - \dfrac{1 \pm \sqrt{4(k^2 + k - q) + 1}}{2}$. From these two cases, it can be inferred that quadratic integers in $\mathbf{Q}(\sqrt{d})$ must be expressible in the form

$$
a + b\omega
$$

where $a$ and $b$ are integers, and

$$
\omega = \begin{cases}
\dfrac{1 + \sqrt{d}}{2}, & d \equiv 1 \pmod 4, \\
\sqrt{d}, & d \equiv 2,3 \pmod 4.
\end{cases}
$$

Conversely, any complex number of this form is a quadratic integer. The representation of each non-rational quadratic integer in this form is unique.

The set of all quadratic integers in the quadratic field $\mathbf{Q}(\sqrt{d})$ is denoted $\mathbf{Z}[\omega]$. Since this set is closed under addition, subtraction, and multiplication, it is also called a **quadratic integer ring**. The rational numbers in the quadratic integer ring are exactly all integers. If we consider the set of all ratios of quadratic integers in $\mathbf{Z}[\omega]$, we obtain the corresponding quadratic field $\mathbf{Q}(\sqrt{d})$.

The trace, norm, and discriminant of quadratic integers are all integers. The minimum value of the discriminants of all quadratic irrationals in the quadratic integer ring $\mathbf{Z}[\omega]$ is also called the discriminant of the corresponding quadratic field $\mathbf{Q}(\sqrt{d})$. When $d \equiv 1 \pmod 4$, the discriminant is $d$; when $d \equiv 2,3 \pmod 4$, the discriminant is $4d$.

### Divisibility, Associates, and Units

Similar to integers, a theory of divisibility can be established for quadratic integers. Of course, the discussion must be carried out within the same quadratic integer ring.

For quadratic integers $\alpha$ and $\beta$ in the quadratic integer ring $\mathbf{Z}[\omega]$, if there exists a quadratic integer $\gamma$ in the same ring such that $\beta = \alpha\gamma$ holds, we say that $\alpha$ divides $\beta$, denoted $\alpha \mid \beta$. Divisibility is a [partial order](../order-theory.md#binary-relation) relation on the quadratic integer ring. If simultaneously $\alpha \mid \beta$ and $\beta \mid \alpha$, then $\alpha$ and $\beta$ can be regarded as the same number in the study of divisibility theory; they are called **associates**. Associate relation is an equivalence relation on the quadratic integer ring.

Analogous to the divisibility theory of integers, the associate relation for integers is being negatives of each other. Divisibility theory usually only needs to consider natural numbers, not negative numbers. For quadratic integers, the associate relation may be somewhat more complex. If quadratic integers $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are associates, then there exist quadratic integers $\gamma$ and $\delta$ such that $\beta = \alpha\gamma$ and $\alpha = \beta\delta$ hold. Therefore, the ratio of $\beta$ and $\alpha$, namely $\gamma$, must be a special quadratic integer, i.e., there exists a quadratic integer $\delta$ such that $\gamma\delta = 1$. Such a quadratic integer $\gamma$ is called a **unit**, also called a reversible element. Two quadratic integers are associates if and only if their ratio is a unit. Therefore, to understand the associate relation on the quadratic integer ring, one must understand the structure of its units.

Since the norm can preserve multiplication, and the norm of a quadratic integer is always an integer, using the norm can transform the divisibility relation of quadratic integers into the divisibility relation of integers. That is, if $\alpha \mid \beta$, then necessarily $N(\alpha) \mid N(\beta)$. Similarly, a quadratic integer $\alpha$ is a unit if and only if its norm $N(\alpha) = \pm 1$. Therefore, to obtain all units in the quadratic integer ring $\mathbf{Z}[\omega]$, one only needs to solve the Diophantine equation:

$$
N(a + b\sqrt{d}) = 1
$$

where the expression for the norm is

$$
N(a + b\sqrt{d}) = \begin{cases}
a^2 + ab + \dfrac{1 - d}{4}b^2, & d \equiv 1 \pmod 4, \\
a^2 - db^2, & d \equiv 2,3 \pmod 4.
\end{cases}
$$

For imaginary quadratic integer rings, i.e., when $d < 0$, since the norm is necessarily a nonnegative integer, it can be easily verified that for all squarefree negative numbers $d \neq -1, -3$, the only solutions are $(a, b) = (\pm 1, 0)$. That is, except for $\mathbf{Z}[\sqrt{-1}]$ and $\mathbf{Z}[\frac{1 + \sqrt{-3}}{2}]$, the units in imaginary quadratic integer rings are only $\pm 1$. Let $\mathrm{i} = \sqrt{-1}$, then the quadratic integer ring $\mathbf{Z}[\mathrm{i}]$ is also called the Gaussian integer ring, and its set of units is $\{\pm 1, \pm \mathrm{i}\}$. Let $\omega = \frac{1 + \sqrt{-3}}{2}$, then the quadratic integer ring $\mathbf{Z}[\omega]$ is also called the Eisenstein integer ring, and its set of units is $\{\pm 1, \pm \omega, \pm \omega^2\}$.

For real quadratic integer rings, i.e., when $d > 0$, the situation is more complex and can be transformed into a discussion of the corresponding [Pell equation](./pell-equation.md). From the related conclusions about the Pell equation, it can be known that the set of all units in this case can be written as $\{\pm u^k : k \in \mathbf{Z}\}$, where $u$ is called the **fundamental unit**. The fundamental unit can be solved through the corresponding Pell equation. The fundamental unit is not unique: if $u$ is a fundamental unit, then its conjugate $\bar{u}$ as well as $-u$ and $-\bar{u}$ are all fundamental units.

The structure of units in quadratic integer rings can be generalized to general [algebraic integer rings](../algebra/field-theory.md#algebraic-extensions). The [Dirichlet unit theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirichlet%27s_unit_theorem) in algebraic number theory states that all units in any algebraic integer ring form a [finitely generated abelian group](../algebra/group-theory.md#finitely-generated-abelian-group), and this theorem also gives the rank of this group.

The content in the divisibility theory of integers, such as the greatest common divisor, Euclidean division, Bezout's theorem, and the unique factorization theorem, can all be generalized to some or all quadratic integer rings. Whether such a generalization can be carried out on a certain quadratic integer ring actually reflects whether the quadratic integer ring is sufficiently similar to the integer ring. The unique factorization theorem does not hold on all quadratic integer rings; even among quadratic integer rings where the unique factorization theorem holds, only some of them allow Euclidean division. For discussions on this part of content, one can refer to the section on [quadratic integer rings](../algebra/ring-theory.md#examples-quadratic-integer-rings) in the ring theory part or related books.

### Unique Factorization

If the unique factorization theorem of integers can be generalized to a quadratic integer ring, it should have the following form: every quadratic integer in the ring $\mathbf{Z}[\omega]$ can be written as a product of irreducible elements, and this decomposition is unique up to associate and order. An [irreducible element](../algebra/ring-theory.md#divisibility-relation) is a quadratic integer that cannot be further decomposed into a product of non-units, similar to prime numbers in the unique factorization theorem of integers. As mentioned earlier, not all quadratic integer rings satisfy the unique factorization theorem.

For example, in $\mathbf{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$, there is the decomposition $9 = 3 \times 3 = (2 + \sqrt{-5}) \times (2 - \sqrt{-5})$, but $3$ and $2 \pm \sqrt{-5}$ are already irreducible elements, so the decomposition is not unique. To show that these three numbers are all irreducible, one can examine their norms: their norms are all $9$, and if they can be decomposed into a product of non-units, then the norm of the factor in the decomposition can only be $3$, but there is no quadratic integer with norm $3$ in $\mathbf{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$.

The main reason that the unique factorization theorem does not hold in general quadratic integer rings is that the decomposition using only quadratic integers is not thorough enough. For example, to decompose the product $abcd$, but the available basic elements are only $\{ab, cd, ac, bd\}$, then the obtained decomposition is naturally not unique; to obtain the unique factorization theorem, one must further consider $\{a, b, c, d\}$. In quadratic integer rings, the structure finer than quadratic integers is the [ideals](../algebra/ring-theory.md#ideals) of the quadratic integer ring. Mapping a quadratic integer to its principal ideal can embed its associate equivalence class into the set of ideals, so the decomposition of integers is just a special case of the decomposition of ideals. If we consider the decomposition of ideals in quadratic integer rings, it can be proved that all ideals in quadratic integer rings can be uniquely decomposed into a product of prime ideals. This shows that all quadratic integer rings are [Dedekind domains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dedekind_domain). More generally, all algebraic integer rings are Dedekind domains.

If the unique factorization theorem holds on a quadratic integer ring, then its prime ideals correspond one-to-one with irreducible elements (up to associate), therefore, for this type of quadratic integer ring, decomposing ideals into prime ideals is equivalent to decomposing quadratic integers into irreducible elements. Moreover, the irreducible elements in this case are also called [prime elements](../algebra/ring-theory.md#divisibility-relation), which correspond to the concept of prime numbers in integers. The following discussion will use prime ideals and related concepts; readers unfamiliar with this concept can replace them word-for-word with prime elements, and these statements will also be correct in cases where the unique factorization theorem holds.

To fully understand the unique factorization on the general quadratic integer ring $\mathbf{Z}[\omega]$, one needs to know what all prime ideals in $\mathbf{Z}[\omega]$ are. First, every prime ideal in $\mathbf{Z}[\omega]$ divides its norm (the principal ideal). Decompose its norm in integers into a product of several prime numbers, then according to the unique factorization theorem, this prime ideal must divide one of these prime factors (the principal ideal). Therefore, prime ideals in $\mathbf{Z}[\omega]$ must be obtained by further decomposing prime numbers in integers. To list all prime ideals in $\mathbf{Z}[\omega]$, one only needs to give the unique decomposition of the prime number $p$ (the principal ideal) in $\mathbf{Z}[\omega)$. Since the norm of the prime number $p$ (the principal ideal) in $\mathbf{Z}[\omega]$ is $p^2$, and its decomposition into prime ideals means that the norm of the prime ideal must be a factor of $p^2$, so it can only be $p$ or $p^2$. This shows that there are only the following three possibilities:

1.  $p$ is **inert** in $\mathbf{Z}[\omega]$, i.e., $(p)$ remains a prime ideal in $\mathbf{Z}[\omega]$;
2.  $p$ **splits** in $\mathbf{Z}[\omega]$, i.e., $(p)$ can be written in $\mathbf{Z}[\omega]$ as the product of two distinct conjugate prime ideals;
3.  $p$ **ramifies** in $\mathbf{Z}[\omega]$, i.e., $(p)$ can be written in $\mathbf{Z}[\omega]$ as the square of some prime ideal.

Furthermore, it can be proved that to determine which of these three cases a prime number $p$ falls into, one only needs to compute the [Kronecker symbol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kronecker_symbol) $\left(\dfrac{D}{p}\right)$ for the discriminant $D$ of the quadratic field containing $\mathbf{Z}[\omega]$ and the prime number $p$. These three cases correspond to the three values of the Kronecker symbol: $-1$, $+1$, and $0$. When $p$ is an odd prime, the Kronecker symbol is the [Legendre symbol](./quad-residue.md#legendre-symbol), and these three cases correspond respectively to $D$ being a [quadratic nonresidue](./quad-residue.md) of $p$, $D$ being a [quadratic residue](./quad-residue.md) of $p$, and $p$ dividing $D$. When $p = 2$, these three cases correspond respectively to $D \equiv \pm 3 \pmod 8$, $D \equiv \pm 1 \pmod 8$, and $2$ dividing $D$.

## Gaussian Integers

In this section, let $\mathrm{i} = \sqrt{-1}$ denote the imaginary unit. The quadratic field $\mathbf{Q}(\mathrm{i})$ is also called the Gaussian field, and it is also a fourth-degree [cyclotomic field](../algebra/field-theory.md#cyclotomic-field). The corresponding quadratic integer ring $\mathbf{Z}[\mathrm{i}]$ is also called the Gaussian integer ring, and its elements are called **Gaussian integers**. The units in Gaussian integers are four in number, namely $\pm 1$ and $\pm \mathrm{i}$, so each nonzero Gaussian integer has four associates (including itself). On the complex plane, Gaussian integers represent all integer lattice points, and the norm of a Gaussian integer $N(a + b\mathrm{i}) = a^2 + b^2$ is the norm on the complex plane.

![](./images/gaussian-integer.svg)

Euclidean division can be performed on Gaussian integers: for Gaussian integers $a$ and $b \neq 0$, there always exist Gaussian integers $q$ and $r$ such that $a = bq + r$ holds, and $N(r) < N(b)$. To compute such Euclidean division, one can first compute $\dfrac{a}{b}$ in $\mathbf{Q}(\mathrm{i})$, then find the nearest integer lattice point in the complex plane as $q$, and then compute $r = a - bq$; the remainder obtained this way always satisfies $N(r) \le \dfrac{1}{2}N(b)$. Using Euclidean division, the Euclidean algorithm and Bezout's theorem can be transferred to Gaussian integers, and then the unique factorization theorem can be established.

### Gaussian Primes

Using the conclusions of the previous section, the prime elements (also called Gaussian primes) in Gaussian integers can be found. Since the discriminant of the Gaussian integer ring is $-4$, and the Kronecker symbol $\left(\dfrac{-4}{n}\right)$ takes the value $0$ on even $n$, and on odd $n$ its value equals $\left(\dfrac{-1}{n}\right) = (-1)^{(n-1)/2}$, therefore we have

$$
\left(\dfrac{-4}{n}\right) = \begin{cases}
+1, & n \equiv 1 \pmod 4, \\
-1, & n \equiv 3 \pmod 4, \\
0, & 2 \mid n.
\end{cases}
$$

So Gaussian primes are of the following three types in total:

1.  Prime numbers of the form $4k + 3$ in the integers;
2.  Two conjugate Gaussian prime factors of prime numbers of the form $4k + 1$ in the integers;
3.  The factor $1 + \mathrm{i}$ of the prime number $2$, whose conjugate is associate to it.

For example, in $\mathbf{Z}[\mathrm{i}]$, there is the decomposition $60 = 2^2 \times 3 \times 5 = -(1 + \mathrm{i})^4 \times 3 \times (2 + \mathrm{i}) \times (2 - \mathrm{i})$.

The discussion here about whether a prime number $p$ can be further decomposed in $\mathbf{Z}[\mathrm{i}]$ is actually equivalent to discussing whether the prime number $p$ can be expressed as a sum of two integer squares $a^2 + b^2$. Therefore, from the conclusions here, it can be deduced that a prime number $p$ can be expressed as a sum of squares if and only if $p = 2$ or $p \equiv 1 \pmod 4$. This is [Fermat's theorem on sums of two squares](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermat%27s_theorem_on_sums_of_two_squares).

### Integer Points on a Circle

On the complex plane, Gaussian integers represent all integer lattice points. The norm of a Gaussian integer is the square of the Euclidean distance from the lattice point to the origin. Therefore, quadratic integers with the same norm correspond to integer points on a circle centered at the origin in the complex plane. That is, the number of integer points on the circle $x^2 + y^2 = n$ is the number of Gaussian integers $x + y\mathrm{i}$ with norm equal to $n$.

To solve the equation $N(x + y\mathrm{i}) = n$, one can consider the decomposition of the Gaussian integer $x + y\mathrm{i}$ into prime factors; then the product of the norms of these prime factors equals $n$. Therefore, as long as one first obtains the factorization of $n$, one can determine what prime factors $x + y\mathrm{i}$ may have based on the prime factors of $n$. Let the prime factorization of the integer $n$ be

$$
n = 2^k p_1^{r_1} \cdots p_\ell^{r_\ell} q_1^{s_1} \cdots q_m^{s_m}
$$

where $p_1, \ldots, p_\ell$ are prime factors of the form $4k + 1$, and $q_1, \ldots, q_m$ are prime factors of the form $4k + 3$.

First, the equation $N(x + y\mathrm{i}) = n$ has a solution if and only if the exponents $s_1, \ldots, s_\ell$ of the prime factors of the form $4k + 3$ in $n$ are all even; this is because $q_1, \ldots, q_m$ are also Gaussian primes, and their norms equal their squares, so in the prime factorization of the norm they must appear in pairs.

Now suppose the equation has a solution. Then the solution must have the form

$$
u(1 + \mathrm{i})^k (a_1 + b_1\mathrm{i})^{r_1^+}(a_1 - b_1\mathrm{i})^{r_1^-} \cdots (a_\ell + b_\ell\mathrm{i})^{r_\ell^+}(a_\ell - b_\ell\mathrm{i})^{r_\ell^-} q_1^{s_1/2} \cdots q_m^{s_m/2}
$$

where $u$ is a unit, $a_j \pm b_j\mathrm{i}$ are the conjugate prime factors of $p_j$ in the Gaussian integer ring, and $r_j^+ + r_j^- = r_j$. Therefore, the number of solutions of the equation is

$$
4(1 + r_1) \cdots (1 + r_\ell)
$$

Let $f(n)$ denote the number of integer solutions of the equation $x^2 + y^2 = n$. When it has a solution, $f(n)$ is given by the above expression; otherwise, $f(n) = 0$. It is easy to verify that $\dfrac{1}{4}f(n)$ is a [multiplicative function](./basic.md#multiplicative-function). The value of a multiplicative function is determined by its values on prime powers. According to the expression of $f(n)$, the values of $\dfrac{1}{4}f(n)$ on prime powers $p^k$ are as follows:

1.  If $p$ is a prime of the form $4k + 3$, then $\dfrac{1}{4}f(1) = 1, \dfrac{1}{4}f(p) = 0, \dfrac{1}{4}f(p^2) = 1, \dfrac{1}{4}f(p^3) = 0, \ldots$;
2.  If $p$ is a prime of the form $4k + 1$, then $\dfrac{1}{4}f(p^k) = k + 1$;
3.  If $p = 2$, then $\dfrac{1}{4}f(2^k) = 1$.

It is easy to verify that these three cases can all be written as

$$
\dfrac{1}{4}f(p^k) = \sum_{j=0}^k \left(\dfrac{-4}{p}\right)^k = \sum_{j=0}^k \left(\dfrac{-4}{p^k}\right) = \sum_{d \mid p^k} \left(\dfrac{-4}{d}\right)
$$

Since the Kronecker symbol $\left(\dfrac{-4}{n}\right)$ is a completely multiplicative function, we can obtain

$$
f(n) = 4\sum_{d \mid n} \left(\dfrac{-4}{d}\right) = 4\sum_{d \mid n} \chi_{4,3}(d)
$$

The notation in the rightmost summation uses the fact that the Kronecker symbol $\left(\dfrac{-4}{n}\right)$ is a real [Dirichlet character](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirichlet_character) modulo $4$.

### Pythagorean Equation

Gaussian integers can be used to find the general solution of the Pythagorean equation. The Pythagorean equation refers to the following quadratic Diophantine equation:

$$
x^2 + y^2 = z^2
$$

Comparing with the content of the previous section, this is equivalent to solving the equation $N(x + y\mathrm{i}) = z^2$. Let $z$ have the prime factorization in $\mathbf{Z}$

$$
z = 2^k p_1^{r_1} \cdots p_\ell^{r_\ell} q_1^{s_1} \cdots q_m^{s_m}
$$

Thus its solution $x + y\mathrm{i}$ has the prime factorization in $\mathbf{Z}[\mathrm{i}]$

$$
x + y\mathrm{i} = u(1 + \mathrm{i})^{2k} (a_1 + b_1\mathrm{i})^{r_1^+}(a_1 - b_1\mathrm{i})^{r_1^-} \cdots (a_\ell + b_\ell\mathrm{i})^{r_\ell^+}(a_\ell - b_\ell\mathrm{i})^{r_\ell^-} q_1^{s_1} \cdots q_m^{s_m}
$$

and $r_j^+ + r_j^- = 2r_j$. One can compute the greatest common divisor of $x + y\mathrm{i}$ and $z$, obtaining the following integer:

$$
\kappa = 2^k p_1^{\min\{r_1^+, r_1^-\}} \cdots p_\ell^{\min\{r_\ell^+, r_\ell^-\}} q_1^{s_1} \cdots q_m^{s_m}
$$

Eliminating this common factor, then $\kappa^{-1}(x + y\mathrm{i})$ contains only prime factors of the form $a_j \pm b_j\mathrm{i}$, the conjugate factors do not appear in pairs, and the exponents $|r_j^+ - r_j^-|$ must be even (since their sum $2r_j$ is even). Therefore, $\kappa^{-1}(x + y\mathrm{i})$ is the square of some quadratic integer $u + v\mathrm{i}$. From this, we obtain the following equations:

$$
x + y\mathrm{i} = \kappa (u + v\mathrm{i})^2, \quad z = \kappa N(u + v\mathrm{i})
$$

In integers $\mathbf{Z}$, this is equivalent to the following system of equations:

$$
x = \kappa(u^2 - v^2), \quad y = 2\kappa uv, \quad z = \kappa(u^2 + v^2)
$$

Conversely, for any integers $u$ and $v$, the above expressions all satisfy the Pythagorean equation. Therefore, this is the general solution of the Pythagorean equation.

From the above process, it can be known that in primitive Pythagorean triples $(x, y, z)$ (i.e., $x, y, z$ have greatest common divisor $1$), $x$ and $y$ must be one odd and one even, $z$ is odd and contains only prime factors of the form $4k + 1$.

Using similar methods, one can also obtain the general solution of the equation $x^2 + y^2 = z^3$, or prove that the equation $x^4 + y^4 = z^4$ has no solution. Of course, using the general solution of the Pythagorean equation and infinite descent, a stronger conclusion can be proved, namely that the equation $x^4 + y^4 = z^2$ has no solution.

## Eisenstein Integers

In this section, let $\omega = \dfrac{-1 + \sqrt{3}\mathrm{i}}{2} = e^{2\pi\mathrm{i}/3}$.[^omega] The quadratic field $\mathbf{Q}(\sqrt{3}\mathrm{i})$ is a third and sixth-degree [cyclotomic field](../algebra/field-theory.md#cyclotomic-field), and the algebraic integers in it are called Eisenstein integers. The ring of all Eisenstein integers $\mathbf{Z}[\omega]$ is called the Eisenstein integer ring. The units in the Eisenstein integer ring are six in number, namely $\pm 1$, $\pm \omega$, and $\pm \omega^2$. On the complex plane, all Eisenstein integers form triangular lattice points. Unlike Gaussian integers, the lattice points here are generally not integer points.

![](./images/eisenstein-integer.svg)

The norm of an Eisenstein integer is

$$
N(a + b\omega) = a^2 - ab + b^2
$$

It is also the square of the distance from the above lattice points on the complex plane to the origin.

Eisenstein integers are also very similar to Gaussian integers. On Eisenstein integers, Euclidean division can also be defined using the norm $N(\cdot)$, and conclusions such as the Euclidean algorithm, Bezout's theorem, and the unique factorization theorem can be established. Similar to the above, the factors of prime numbers in the Eisenstein integer ring can be derived. To do this, note that the discriminant of $\mathbf{Z}[\omega]$ is $-3$, and the Kronecker symbol

$$
\left(\dfrac{-3}{n}\right) = \begin{cases}
+1, & n \equiv 1 \pmod 3, \\
-1, & n \equiv 2 \pmod 3, \\
0, & 3 \mid n,
\end{cases}
$$

Therefore, the prime elements in the Eisenstein integer ring, i.e., Eisenstein primes, are of the following three types:

1.  Prime numbers of the form $3k + 2$ in the integers, i.e., $2$ and prime numbers of the form $6k + 5$;
2.  Two conjugate Gaussian prime factors of prime numbers of the form $3k + 1$ (i.e., prime numbers of the form $6k + 1$) in the integers;
3.  The factor $(3 + \sqrt{3}\mathrm{i})/2$ of the prime number $3$, whose conjugate is associate to it.

Using the method in the previous section, the number of Eisenstein integers with norm $n$ can be found. Let this number be $f(n)$, then it equals

$$
f(n) = 6\sum_{d \mid n} \left(\dfrac{-3}{d}\right) = 6\sum_{d \mid n} \chi_{3,2}(d)
$$

where $\chi_{3,2}(n) = \left(\dfrac{-3}{n}\right)$ is a real Dirichlet character modulo $3$. This expression shows that such Eisenstein integers exist if and only if all prime factors of the form $3k + 2$ in $n$ have even exponents.

Using the norm formula from earlier, $f(n)$ is also the number of solutions to the Diophantine equation $x^2 - xy + y^2 = n$ or the Diophantine equation $x^2 + xy + y^2 = n$. Its corresponding geometric meaning is the number of integer points on the slanted ellipse $x^2 \pm xy + y^2 = n$.

Closely related to these equations is another Diophantine equation $x^2 + 3y^2 = n$, whose integer solutions correspond to integer points on a standard ellipse. By making the variable substitution $x = (u + v)/2$ and $y = (u - v)/2$, it can be transformed into the already solved equation $u^2 - uv + v^2 = n$. However, while integer solutions to $x^2 + 3y^2 = n$ always correspond to integer solutions to $u^2 - uv + v^2 = n$, the reverse is not necessarily true. If $n$ is even, then $u$ and $v$ must both be even, which means $x$ and $y$ must be integers, so the number of integer solutions to $x^2 + 3y^2 = n$ is still $f(n)$. However, if $n$ is odd, then $u$ and $v$ may be one odd and one even, or both odd, so the corresponding $x$ and $y$ may both be half-integers or both integers. This case requires a more detailed discussion.

When a integer solution $(u, v)$ of the equation $u^2 - uv + v^2 = n$ is known, the integer solutions corresponding to Eisenstein integers associated with $u + v\omega$ (including itself) are respectively

$$
(u, v), (u - v, u), (-v, u - v), (-u, -v), (v - u, -u), (v, v - u)
$$

Since among $u, v, u - v$, as long as they are not all even, there must be two odd and one even, among these six sets of integer solutions, there must be two sets that are all odd and four sets that are one odd and one even. This shows that when $n$ is odd, among the integer solutions of $u^2 - uv + v^2 = n$, only $\dfrac{1}{3}$ are all odd, and only then can they correspond to integer solutions of $x^2 + 3y^2 = n$. Therefore, when $n$ is odd, the number of integer solutions to $x^2 + 3y^2 = n$ is $\dfrac{1}{3}f(n)$.

Finally, similar to the method of solving the Pythagorean equation, Eisenstein integers can be used to solve the following Diophantine equations:

$$
x^2 - xy + y^2 = z^2
$$

$$
x^2 + xy + y^2 = z^2
$$

$$
x^2 + 3y^2 = z^2
$$

$$
x^2 + 3y^2 = z^3
$$

Their general solutions are not detailed here. Similar methods can also be used to prove that $x^3 + y^3 = z^3$ has no solution.
