    Inner and Outer Products

    This article introduces simple operations between vectors.

    Before this article, a note on translation. Due to historical reasons, mathematics and physics have various translations for "inner product" and "outer product".

    In physics, they are generally translated as "scalar product" and "vector product", indicating that the results are scalar and vector quantities. High school mathematics textbooks also use "dot product" and "cross product" with this approach.

    In mathematics, they are usually translated as "inner product" and "outer product", which are direct translations of the two terms. "Dot product" and "cross product" are colloquial names derived from the operation symbols, and are also common.

    In the "dot product" operation, the dot symbol is often omitted. In linear algebra, it's often treated as matrix multiplication without the dot symbol.

    Inner Product

    The concept of inner product applies to vectors of any dimension.

    Definition

    There are different but equivalent ways to define inner product. Some are introduced below.

    Geometric Definition

    In 𝑛n-dimensional Euclidean space 𝐑𝑛Rn, given two vectors 𝒂,𝒃a,b, with angle 𝜃θ between them:

    𝒂⋅𝒃=|𝒂||𝒃|cos⁡𝜃a⋅b=|a||b|cos⁡θ

    This is the inner product (also called dot product or scalar product) of the two vectors. Here |𝒃|cos⁡𝜃|b|cos⁡θ is called the projection of 𝒃b onto the direction of 𝒂a. The geometric meaning of inner product is: 𝒂 ⋅𝒃a⋅b equals the product of the magnitude of 𝒂a and the projection of 𝒃b onto 𝒂a.

    Algebraic Definition

    In 𝑛n-dimensional Euclidean space 𝐑𝑛Rn, given two vectors 𝒂 =(𝑎1,𝑎2,…,𝑎𝑛),𝒃 =(𝑏1,𝑏2,…,𝑏𝑛)a=(a1,a2,…,an),b=(b1,b2,…,bn):

    𝒂⋅𝒃=𝑛∑𝑖=1𝑎𝑖𝑏𝑖a⋅b=∑i=1naibi

    This is the inner product (also called dot product or scalar product) of the two vectors. The geometric and algebraic definitions are equivalent in Euclidean space, and the latter is more convenient to use.

    When it does not cause confusion, the dot in inner product can be omitted. If a vector has a superscript 22 in the upper right corner, it represents the abbreviation of the inner product of the vector with itself, i.e., the square of the vector's magnitude, omitting the magnitude symbol. This superscript 22 cannot be interpreted as the square of the vector, because the result of a vector inner product is a scalar, and there is no inner product of any number of vectors other than 22. Similarly, the square of the square of the vector's magnitude cannot be abbreviated as superscript 44, but rather the result of superscript 22 must be treated as a whole, and so on.

    Properties

    One can see that the result of an inner product is a scalar. Its special property is that it is a bilinear operation that is linear in each argument separately. Specifically, inner product satisfies:

    (𝒂+𝒃)⋅𝒄=𝒂⋅𝒄+𝒃⋅𝒄𝒂⋅(𝒃+𝒄)=𝒂⋅𝒃+𝒂⋅𝒄(𝜆𝒂)⋅𝒃=𝜆(𝒂⋅𝒃)𝒂⋅(𝜆𝒃)=𝜆(𝒂⋅𝒃)(a+b)⋅c=a⋅c+b⋅ca⋅(b+c)=a⋅b+a⋅c(λa)⋅b=λ(a⋅b)a⋅(λb)=λ(a⋅b)

    Inner product also satisfies the commutative law:

    𝒂⋅𝒃=𝒃⋅𝒂a⋅b=b⋅a

    Applications

    Some common applications of inner product are introduced below.

    1. Determining perpendicular vectors:

      𝒂⟂𝒃⟺𝒂⋅𝒃=0a⟂b⟺a⋅b=0

      That is, the inner product of two perpendicular vectors is 00; the inner product of any vector with the zero vector is 00. If we use zero inner product as the definition of perpendicularity, we can conclude that the zero vector is perpendicular to any vector.

    2. Determining collinear vectors:

      ∃𝜆∈𝐑(𝒂=𝜆𝒃)⟺|𝒂⋅𝒃|=|𝒂||𝒃|∃λ∈R(a=λb)⟺|a⋅b|=|a||b|

    3. Computing the magnitude of a vector:

      |𝒂|=√𝒂⋅𝒂|a|=a⋅a

    4. Computing the angle between two vectors:

      𝜃=arccos⁡𝒂⋅𝒃|𝒂||𝒃|θ=arccos⁡a⋅b|a||b|

    2×2 and 3×3 Determinants

    The 2 ×22×2 and 3 ×33×3 determinants can be defined as special simple cases of determinants. In the final part of calculus, Green's formula uses 2 ×22×2 determinants, Gauss's formula uses dot products, and Stokes' formula uses 3 ×33×3 determinants.

    The 2 ×22×2 determinant can be viewed as a function of four numbers:

    ∣𝑎𝑏𝑐𝑑∣=𝑎𝑑−𝑏𝑐|abcd|=ad−bc

    The 3 ×33×3 determinant can be viewed as a function of nine numbers:

    ∣𝑎𝑏𝑐𝑑𝑒𝑓𝑔ℎ𝑖∣=𝑎𝑒𝑖+𝑑ℎ𝑐+𝑔𝑏𝑓−𝑎ℎ𝑓−𝑑𝑏𝑖−𝑔𝑒𝑐|abcdefghi|=aei+dhc+gbf−ahf−dbi−gec

    A special memory method uses the "diagonal rule", which only applies to 2 ×22×2 and 3 ×33×3 determinants.

    Special note: A 4 ×44×4 determinant expands to 24 terms, and the term from the secondary diagonal has a positive sign. If one attempts to apply the "diagonal rule" for 3 ×33×3 determinants to higher-order ones, not only are there insufficient terms, but the sign of the secondary diagonal term is also incorrect. Therefore, the "diagonal rule" for 3 ×33×3 determinants does not apply to higher-order determinants, and higher-order determinants are not suitable for direct expansion.

    Cross Product

    The cross product is an operation specific to three-dimensional vectors.

    In physics, three-dimensional vectors are considered by default as vectors associated with spatial position, and are always in bold. However, in relativity, four-dimensional vectors are not in bold but use special notations and subscripts.

    In linear algebra, all vectors are in bold, and since it's cumbersome and most operations in linear algebra involve vectors and matrices, which rarely cause ambiguity, the vector notation can be omitted when writing by hand.

    Definition

    There are different but equivalent ways to define cross product. Some are introduced below.

    Geometric Definition

    In three-dimensional Euclidean space 𝐑3R3, define the cross product of vectors 𝒂,𝒃a,b as a vector, denoted 𝒂 ×𝒃a×b, with magnitude and direction defined as follows:

    1. |𝒂 ×𝒃| =|𝒂||𝒃|sin⁡⟨𝒂,𝒃⟩|a×b|=|a||b|sin⁡⟨a,b⟩;
    2. 𝒂 ×𝒃a×b is perpendicular to both 𝒂a and 𝒃b, and the direction of 𝒂,𝒃,𝒂 ×𝒃a,b,a×b follows the right-hand rule.

    Notice that the magnitude of the cross product, recalling the triangle area formula 𝑆 =12𝑎𝑏sin⁡𝐶S=12absin⁡C, reveals the geometric meaning: |𝒂 ×𝒃||a×b| is the area of the parallelogram with 𝒂,𝒃a,b as adjacent sides.

    Algebraic Definition

    In three-dimensional Euclidean space 𝐑3R3, define vectors 𝒂 =(𝑥1,𝑦1,𝑧1),𝒃 =(𝑥2,𝑦2,𝑧2)a=(x1,y1,z1),b=(x2,y2,z2). The cross product is a vector 𝒄c, denoted 𝒄 =𝒂 ×𝒃c=a×b, which can be expressed using a 3 ×33×3 determinant:

    ∣𝒊𝒋𝒌𝑥1𝑦1𝑧1𝑥2𝑦2𝑧2∣|ijkx1y1z1x2y2z2|

    where 𝒊,𝒋,𝒌i,j,k are unit vectors pointing along the 𝑥,𝑦,𝑧x,y,z axes, written at the corresponding coordinate positions. Expanding gives:

    𝒄=𝒂×𝒃=(𝑦1𝑧2−𝑦2𝑧1)𝒊+(𝑧1𝑥2−𝑧2𝑥1)𝒋+(𝑥1𝑦2−𝑥2𝑦1)𝒌=(𝑦1𝑧2−𝑦2𝑧1,𝑧1𝑥2−𝑧2𝑥1,𝑥1𝑦2−𝑥2𝑦1)c=a×b=(y1z2−y2z1)i+(z1x2−z2x1)j+(x1y2−x2y1)k=(y1z2−y2z1,z1x2−z2x1,x1y2−x2y1)

    Properties

    1. The cross product is bilinear, linear in each argument separately. Specifically, the cross product satisfies:

      (𝒂+𝒃)×𝒄=𝒂×𝒄+𝒃×𝒄𝒂×(𝒃+𝒄)=𝒂×𝒃+𝒂×𝒄(𝜆𝒂)×𝒃=𝜆(𝒂×𝒃)𝒂×(𝜆𝒃)=𝜆(𝒂×𝒃)(a+b)×c=a×c+b×ca×(b+c)=a×b+a×c(λa)×b=λ(a×b)a×(λb)=λ(a×b)

      The first two properties are also called the distributive law, i.e., cross product distributes over vector addition.

    2. The cross product satisfies the anticommutative law:

      𝒂×𝒃=−𝒃×𝒂a×b=−b×a

    3. From the geometric definitions of inner and cross product above:

      |𝒂×𝒃|=|𝒂||𝒃|sin⁡⟨𝒂,𝒃⟩𝒂⋅𝒃=|𝒂||𝒃|cos⁡𝜃=|𝒂||𝒃|cos⁡⟨𝒂,𝒃⟩|a×b|=|a||b|sin⁡⟨a,b⟩a⋅b=|a||b|cos⁡θ=|a||b|cos⁡⟨a,b⟩

      We can write the identity:

      (𝒂×𝒃)⋅(𝒂×𝒃)=|𝒂|2|𝒃|2−(𝒂⋅𝒃)2(a×b)⋅(a×b)=|a|2|b|2−(a⋅b)2

    4. The cross product satisfies the Jacobi identity:

      𝒂×(𝒃×𝒄)+𝒃×(𝒄×𝒂)+𝒄×(𝒂×𝒃)=𝟎a×(b×c)+b×(c×a)+c×(a×b)=0

    Applications

    Some common applications of cross product are introduced below.

    1. Determining whether two vectors are collinear:

      ∃𝜆∈𝐑(𝒂=𝜆𝒃)⟺𝒂×𝒃=𝟎∃λ∈R(a=λb)⟺a×b=0

      That is, the cross product of two collinear three-dimensional vectors is 𝟎0; the cross product of a three-dimensional vector with itself is 𝟎0; the cross product of a three-dimensional vector with the zero vector is 𝟎0. If we use zero cross product as the definition of collinearity, we can conclude that the zero vector is collinear with any vector.

    2. Computing the area of the parallelogram spanned by two vectors:

      𝑆⟨𝒂,𝒃⟩=|𝒂×𝒃|S⟨a,b⟩=|a×b|

    Two-Dimensional Vector Case

    For two-dimensional vectors, cross product cannot be directly computed, but the area of the parallelogram spanned by two vectors can still be computed:

    Let 𝒂 =(𝑚,𝑛),𝒃 =(𝑝,𝑞)a=(m,n),b=(p,q). Extend the plane Cartesian coordinate system to a spatial Cartesian coordinate system, with the original plane in the 𝑥𝑂𝑦xOy plane of the new system, and original coordinates (𝑚,𝑛)(m,n) and (𝑝,𝑞)(p,q) become (𝑚,𝑛,0)(m,n,0) and (𝑝,𝑞,0)(p,q,0).

    Then the cross product of the two vectors is (0,0,𝑚𝑞 −𝑛𝑝)(0,0,mq−np), so the area of the parallelogram is |𝑚𝑞 −𝑛𝑝||mq−np|, which can be viewed as the absolute value of the 2 ×22×2 determinant.
