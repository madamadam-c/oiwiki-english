    Jordan Normal Form

    Jordan Decomposition

    Let 𝑇T be a linear transformation on an 𝑛n-dimensional space 𝑉V. If the minimal polynomial of 𝑇T is:

    𝑚𝐴(𝜆)=(𝜆−𝜆1)𝑟1(𝜆−𝜆2)𝑟2⋯(𝜆−𝜆𝑘)𝑟𝑘mA(λ)=(λ−λ1)r1(λ−λ2)r2⋯(λ−λk)rk

    Then by the primary decomposition theorem, the space 𝑉V can be decomposed into a direct sum of subspaces:

    𝑉=𝑉1⊕𝑉2⊕⋯⊕𝑉𝑘V=V1⊕V2⊕⋯⊕Vk

    where 𝑉𝑖 =𝑁((𝐴−𝜆𝑖𝐼)𝑟𝑖)Vi=N((A−λiI)ri), 𝐴A being the matrix corresponding to 𝑇T, and these subspaces are all invariant under 𝑇T.

    Let the transformation 𝑇𝑖Ti be the projection of 𝑉V onto the subspace 𝑉𝑖Vi, i.e., construct a polynomial 𝑢𝑖(𝑇)ui(T) such that:

    • 𝑇𝑖=𝑢𝑖(𝑇)𝑚𝐴(𝑇)(𝑇−𝜆𝑖𝑇𝑒)𝑟𝑖Ti=ui(T)mA(T)(T−λiTe)ri
    • 𝑇1+𝑇2+⋯+𝑇𝑘=𝑇𝑒T1+T2+⋯+Tk=Te

    where 𝑇𝑒Te denotes the identity transformation on 𝑉V. Then we have the properties:

    • The restriction of 𝑇𝑖Ti to 𝑉𝑖Vi, denoted 𝑇𝑖|𝑉𝑖Ti|Vi, is the identity transformation on 𝑉𝑖Vi.
    • If 𝑖 ≠𝑗i≠j, the restriction of 𝑇𝑖Ti to 𝑉𝑗Vj, denoted 𝑇𝑖|𝑉𝑗Ti|Vj, is the zero transformation on 𝑉𝑗Vj.

    Thus, the transformation 𝑇𝑖Ti maps every vector 𝜉ξ in 𝑉V to its component in 𝑉𝑖Vi.

    Construct the transformation:

    𝑇𝐷=𝜆1𝑇1+𝜆2𝑇2+⋯+𝜆𝑘𝑇𝑘TD=λ1T1+λ2T2+⋯+λkTk

    Since each 𝑇𝑖Ti is a polynomial in 𝑇T, 𝑇𝐷TD is also a polynomial in 𝑇T, so each subspace 𝑉𝑖Vi is invariant under 𝑇𝐷TD.

    From the above, the restriction of 𝑇𝐷TD to 𝑉𝑖Vi, denoted 𝑇𝐷|𝑉𝑖TD|Vi, is a homothety on 𝑉𝑖Vi with ratio 𝜆𝑖λi. Therefore, 𝑇𝐷TD is diagonalizable.

    Construct:

    𝑇𝑁=𝑇−𝑇𝐷TN=T−TD

    Then 𝑇𝑁TN is also a polynomial in 𝑇T, so each subspace 𝑉𝑖Vi is invariant under 𝑇𝑁TN. For any vector 𝜉𝑖ξi in 𝑉𝑖Vi, we have:

    𝑇𝑁𝑟𝑖(𝜉𝑖)=𝑇−𝑇𝐷𝑟𝑖(𝜉𝑖)=𝑇−𝜆𝑖𝑇𝑖𝑟𝑖(𝜉𝑖)=0TNri(ξi)=T−TDri(ξi)=T−λiTiri(ξi)=0

    Let 𝑟r be the maximum of all 𝑟𝑖ri. Then for any vector 𝜉ξ in 𝑉V, 𝑇𝑟𝑁TNr maps 𝜉ξ to the zero vector. Therefore, 𝑇𝑁TN is a nilpotent transformation.

    Thus, every transformation 𝑇T on 𝑉V can be written as:

    𝑇=𝑇𝐷+𝑇𝑁T=TD+TN

    where 𝑇𝐷TD is diagonalizable and 𝑇𝑁TN is nilpotent. Since 𝑇𝐷TD and 𝑇𝑁TN are both polynomials in 𝑇T, their product commutes:

    𝑇𝐷𝑇𝑁=𝑇𝑁𝑇𝐷TDTN=TNTD

    Theorem: Let 𝑇1T1 and 𝑇2T2 be two diagonalizable transformations on 𝑉V such that 𝑇1𝑇2 =𝑇2𝑇1T1T2=T2T1. Then there exists a basis such that the matrices of both 𝑇1T1 and 𝑇2T2 with respect to this basis are diagonal.

    Theorem: Let 𝑇T be a linear transformation on an 𝑛n-dimensional space 𝑉V. Then there exists a diagonalizable transformation 𝑇𝐷TD and a nilpotent transformation 𝑇𝑁TN such that:

    • 𝑇=𝑇𝐷+𝑇𝑁T=TD+TN
    • 𝑇𝐷𝑇𝑁=𝑇𝑁𝑇𝐷TDTN=TNTD

    They are both polynomials in 𝑇T, and are uniquely determined by 𝑇T.

    This decomposition is called the Jordan decomposition of 𝑇T, where 𝑇𝐷TD is called the diagonalizable part of 𝑇T, and 𝑇𝑁TN is called the nilpotent part of 𝑇T.

    Similarly, there is the Jordan decomposition of matrices:

    Theorem: Let 𝐴A be an 𝑛 ×𝑛n×n matrix. Then there exists a diagonalizable matrix 𝐷D and a nilpotent matrix 𝑁N such that:

    • 𝐴=𝐷+𝑁A=D+N
    • 𝐷𝑁=𝑁𝐷DN=ND

    They are both polynomials in 𝐴A, and are uniquely determined by 𝐴A.

    This decomposition is called the Jordan decomposition of 𝐴A, where 𝐷D is called the diagonalizable part of 𝐴A, and 𝑁N is called the nilpotent part of 𝐴A.

    Lambda Matrices

    The following introduces a more general class of matrices containing a variable parameter 𝜆λ, not merely a table of numbers. This discussion is more general than that of matrices over a field of numbers.

    For 𝜆λ-matrices, the corresponding field becomes the field of rational functions in one variable 𝜆λ.

    A matrix whose entries are polynomials in 𝜆λ is called a 𝜆λ-matrix, denoted 𝐴(𝜆)A(λ).

    Since a polynomial field contains a number field, numeric matrices are special cases of 𝜆λ-matrices. In particular, the characteristic matrix 𝜆𝐼 −𝐴λI−A of a numeric matrix 𝐴A is a 𝜆λ-matrix.

    Elementary Operations on Lambda Matrices

    For 𝜆λ-matrices, addition, subtraction, multiplication, and elementary operations can be defined similarly. For square 𝜆λ-matrices, determinant, minor, and cofactor can also be defined.

    For 𝜆λ-matrices, elementary operations are mostly the same as for numeric matrices, except that the row-addition operation is modified (taking row operations as an example):

    • Multiply a row by a polynomial 𝜑(𝜆)φ(λ) in 𝜆λ and add to another row.

    Note that the row-scaling operation is not modified. This is because row addition does not change the determinant, while row scaling does. To preserve the rank properties in the polynomial field, the determinant can only be changed over a number field.

    The corresponding elementary matrices are modified accordingly.

    It is easy to see that the determinants of the three types of elementary matrices are all nonzero constants, hence all are full rank. Therefore, left or right multiplication by them does not change the rank of a 𝜆λ-matrix.

    If 𝐴(𝜆)A(λ) can be transformed into 𝐵(𝜆)B(λ) through a finite number of elementary operations, then 𝐴(𝜆)A(λ) and 𝐵(𝜆)B(λ) are said to be equivalent.

    For 𝜆λ-matrices, equivalent implies same rank. The converse is not true, which differs from numeric matrices.

    Smith Normal Form

    Theorem: Let the rank of a 𝜆λ-matrix be 𝑟r. Then 𝐴(𝜆)A(λ) is always equivalent to:

    (𝐷(𝜆)000)(D(λ)000)

    where:

    𝐷(𝜆)=⎛⎜ ⎜ ⎜⎝𝑑1(𝜆)⋱𝑑𝑟(𝜆)⎞⎟ ⎟ ⎟⎠D(λ)=(d1(λ)⋱dr(λ))

    Each 𝑑𝑖(𝜆)di(λ) is a monic polynomial, and consecutive polynomials satisfy the divisibility relation 𝑑𝑖(𝜆)|𝑑𝑖+1(𝜆)di(λ)|di+1(λ).

    This normal form is called the Smith normal form, and 𝑑𝑖(𝜆)di(λ) are called invariant factors.

    The specific method to compute the Smith normal form is: eliminate from the top-left to bottom-right, each time taking the greatest common divisor of all remaining polynomials in the top-left corner, and using that element to zero out the rest of that row and column.

    Theorem: The condition that 𝐴(𝜆)A(λ) and 𝐵(𝜆)B(λ) are equivalent is equivalent to the condition that 𝐴(𝜆)A(λ) and 𝐵(𝜆)B(λ) have exactly the same invariant factors.

    Elementary Divisors

    By the fundamental theorem of algebra, let the invariant factors of 𝐴(𝜆)A(λ) be 𝑑1(𝜆),𝑑2(𝜆),⋯,𝑑𝑚(𝜆)d1(λ),d2(λ),⋯,dm(λ), with factorization:

    𝑑𝑖(𝜆)=(𝜆−𝜆1)𝑒𝑖1(𝜆−𝜆2)𝑒𝑖2⋯(𝜆−𝜆𝑆)𝑒𝑖𝑆di(λ)=(λ−λ1)ei1(λ−λ2)ei2⋯(λ−λS)eiS

    where 𝜆1,⋯,𝜆𝑆λ1,⋯,λS are distinct. Since:

    𝑑𝑖(𝜆)|𝑑𝑖+1(𝜆)di(λ)|di+1(λ)

    the exponents 𝑒1𝑗,𝑒2𝑗,⋯,𝑒𝑚𝑗e1j,e2j,⋯,emj are non-decreasing, and the last factor 𝑑𝑚(𝜆)dm(λ) has all exponents nonzero.

    All factors with positive exponent in the above expression are collectively called the elementary divisors of 𝐴(𝜆)A(λ).

    Note that elementary divisors count multiplicities. If for some 𝑗j, the exponent 𝑒𝑖𝑗eij appears multiple times, the corresponding elementary divisor (𝜆−𝜆𝑗)𝑒𝑖𝑗(λ−λj)eij should also appear the same number of times.

    The previous theorem states that 𝐴(𝜆)A(λ) and 𝐵(𝜆)B(λ) are equivalent if and only if they have exactly the same invariant factors. Same invariant factors obviously imply same elementary divisors, but not conversely. In fact, we have:

    Theorem: 𝐴(𝜆)A(λ) and 𝐵(𝜆)B(λ) having exactly the same invariant factors is equivalent to having exactly the same elementary divisors and rank.

    Thus, "exactly the same elementary divisors and rank" is also a criterion for equivalence of 𝜆λ-matrices.

    During elementary operations, one can also first transform 𝐴(𝜆)A(λ) to a diagonal matrix, then find the elementary divisors and rank, and finally get the invariant factors for the normal form. There is:

    Theorem: Let 𝐴(𝜆)A(λ) be equivalent to the diagonal matrix:

    diag⁡{𝑓1(𝜆),𝑓2(𝜆),⋯,𝑓𝑟(𝜆),0,⋯,0}diag⁡{f1(λ),f2(λ),⋯,fr(λ),0,⋯,0}

    Then the factors (𝜆−𝜆𝑗)𝑒𝑖𝑗(λ−λj)eij of 𝑓1(𝜆),𝑓2(𝜆),⋯,𝑓𝑟(𝜆)f1(λ),f2(λ),⋯,fr(λ), where each 𝑓𝑖fi is a product of linear factors, together form the elementary divisors of 𝐴(𝜆)A(λ).

    The specific method to construct invariant factors from elementary divisors and rank is: first classify the elementary divisors by factors, arrange them in a table, put factors of the same type in the same row in descending powers, fill the columns to rank 𝑟r with 1's, then the product of each column forms an invariant factor.

    Application to Characteristic Matrices

    If 𝐴A and 𝐵B are numeric matrices, then their characteristic matrices are 𝜆λ-matrices. We have:

    Theorem: Two numeric matrices 𝐴A and 𝐵B are similar if and only if their characteristic matrices 𝜆𝐼 −𝐴λI−A and 𝜆𝐼 −𝐵λI−B are equivalent.

    Since the characteristic matrix 𝜆𝐼 −𝐴λI−A has exactly 𝑛n entries of 𝜆λ on the main diagonal, its rank is 𝑛n. From the above, characteristic matrices of same-sized numeric matrices always have the same rank, so we have the equivalence:

    Numeric matrices 𝐴A and 𝐵B are similar if and only if their characteristic matrices 𝜆𝐼 −𝐴λI−A and 𝜆𝐼 −𝐵λI−B have exactly the same elementary divisors.

    For the characteristic matrix 𝜆𝐼 −𝐴λI−A, elementary operations preserve equivalence, so do not change the rank.

    Observing the three types of elementary operations, since the only modified operation (row addition) does not change the determinant, in fact all three types of elementary operations only change the determinant polynomial by a constant factor, thus do not change the factorization or degree of the determinant polynomial.

    Therefore, the determinant of the characteristic matrix 𝜆𝐼 −𝐴λI−A is an 𝑛n-degree polynomial. After transforming to Smith normal form via elementary operations, since the rank is 𝑛n, the determinant equals the product of all invariant factors on the main diagonal, which also equals the product of all elementary divisors. Thus, the sum of the degrees of all elementary divisors of 𝜆𝐼 −𝐴λI−A equals 𝑛n.

    Jordan Normal Form

    A matrix

    ⎛⎜ ⎜ ⎜ ⎜ ⎜ ⎜ ⎜ ⎜ ⎜ ⎜ ⎜ ⎜ ⎜⎝𝜆10⋯000𝜆1⋯0000𝜆⋯00⋮⋮⋮⋮⋮000⋯𝜆1000⋯0𝜆⎞⎟ ⎟ ⎟ ⎟ ⎟ ⎟ ⎟ ⎟ ⎟ ⎟ ⎟ ⎟ ⎟⎠(λ10⋯000λ1⋯0000λ⋯00⋮⋮⋮⋮⋮000⋯λ1000⋯0λ)

    with all elements on the main diagonal equal to 𝜆λ, all elements immediately above the main diagonal equal to 11, and all other elements 00, is called a Jordan matrix (or Jordan block) associated with 𝜆λ.

    Clearly, a nilpotent Jordan matrix is a special case of a Jordan matrix, namely when 𝜆 =0λ=0.

    Theorem: Let 𝑇T be a transformation on an 𝑛n-dimensional space 𝑉V, and 𝜆1,⋯,𝜆𝑘λ1,⋯,λk be all distinct eigenvalues of 𝑇T. Then there exists a basis such that the matrix of 𝑇T with respect to this basis has the form:

    ⎛⎜ ⎜ ⎜ ⎜ ⎜ ⎜⎝𝐵10𝐵2⋱0𝐵𝑘⎞⎟ ⎟ ⎟ ⎟ ⎟ ⎟⎠(B10B2⋱0Bk)

    where

    𝐵𝑖=⎛⎜ ⎜ ⎜ ⎜ ⎜ ⎜⎝𝐽𝑖10𝐽𝑖2⋱0𝐽𝑖𝑠𝑖⎞⎟ ⎟ ⎟ ⎟ ⎟ ⎟⎠Bi=(Ji10Ji2⋱0Jisi)

    and 𝐽𝑖1,⋯,𝐽𝑖𝑠𝑖Ji1,⋯,Jisi are all Jordan blocks associated with 𝜆𝑖λi.

    The proof proceeds as follows. First, according to the minimal polynomial:

    𝑚𝐴(𝜆)=(𝜆−𝜆1)𝑟1(𝜆−𝜆2)𝑟2⋯(𝜆−𝜆𝑘)𝑟𝑘mA(λ)=(λ−λ1)r1(λ−λ2)r2⋯(λ−λk)rk

    we have the primary decomposition:

    𝑉=𝑉1⊕𝑉2⊕⋯⊕𝑉𝑘V=V1⊕V2⊕⋯⊕Vk

    where:

    𝑉𝑖=𝑁((𝐴−𝜆𝑖𝐼)𝑟𝑖)Vi=N((A−λiI)ri)

    and 𝐴A is the matrix corresponding to 𝑇T.

    Let 𝑆𝑖Si be the restriction of 𝑇T to 𝑉𝑖Vi, denoted 𝑇|𝑉𝑖T|Vi. Next, we attempt to perform Jordan decomposition on each 𝑆𝑖Si.

    Let 𝑇𝑒Te be the identity transformation on 𝑉V. Unlike the previous Jordan decomposition, denote 𝑇𝑖Ti as the nilpotent part in the Jordan decomposition of 𝑆𝑖Si:

    𝑆𝑖=𝜆𝑖𝑇𝑒+𝑇𝑖Si=λiTe+Ti

    Then 𝑇𝑖Ti is a nilpotent transformation on subspace 𝑉𝑖Vi, which is indeed the restriction (𝑇−𝜆𝑖𝑇𝑒)|𝑉𝑖(T−λiTe)|Vi.

    The subspace 𝑉𝑖Vi can be decomposed into a direct sum of cyclic subspaces for the nilpotent transformation 𝑇𝑖Ti:

    𝑉𝑖=𝑊𝑖1⊕𝑊𝑖2⊕⋯⊕𝑊𝑖𝑠𝑖Vi=Wi1⊕Wi2⊕⋯⊕Wisi

    In each cyclic subspace 𝑊𝑖𝑗Wij, take a cyclic basis and arrange in reverse order to form a basis of 𝑉𝑖Vi. Then the matrix of 𝑇𝑖Ti with respect to this basis has the form:

    𝑁𝑖=⎛⎜ ⎜ ⎜ ⎜ ⎜ ⎜⎝𝑁𝑖10𝑁𝑖2⋱0𝑁𝑖𝑠𝑖⎞⎟ ⎟ ⎟ ⎟ ⎟ ⎟⎠Ni=(Ni10Ni2⋱0Nisi)

    All 𝑁𝑖𝑗Nij are nilpotent Jordan blocks. Then with respect to the basis chosen for 𝑉𝑖Vi, the matrix of 𝑆𝑖Si is:

    𝐵𝑖=⎛⎜ ⎜ ⎜ ⎜ ⎜ ⎜⎝𝜆𝑖0𝜆𝑖⋱0𝜆𝑖⎞⎟ ⎟ ⎟ ⎟ ⎟ ⎟⎠+⎛⎜ ⎜ ⎜ ⎜ ⎜ ⎜⎝𝑁𝑖10𝑁𝑖2⋱0𝑁𝑖𝑠𝑖⎞⎟ ⎟ ⎟ ⎟ ⎟ ⎟⎠=⎛⎜ ⎜ ⎜ ⎜ ⎜ ⎜⎝𝐽𝑖10𝐽𝑖2⋱0𝐽𝑖𝑠𝑖⎞⎟ ⎟ ⎟ ⎟ ⎟ ⎟⎠Bi=(λi0λi⋱0λi)+(Ni10Ni2⋱0Nisi)=(Ji10Ji2⋱0Jisi)

    Here 𝐽𝑖1,𝐽𝑖2,⋯,𝐽𝑖𝑠𝑖Ji1,Ji2,⋯,Jisi are all Jordan blocks associated with 𝜆𝑖λi.

    For each subspace 𝑉𝑖Vi, choose a basis as above. Putting them together forms a basis of 𝑉V. Then the matrix of 𝑇T with respect to this basis exactly has the form prescribed in the theorem.

    A matrix of the form:

    ⎛⎜ ⎜ ⎜ ⎜ ⎜ ⎜⎝𝐽10𝐽2⋱0𝐽𝑚⎞⎟ ⎟ ⎟ ⎟ ⎟ ⎟⎠(J10J2⋱0Jm)

    where each 𝐽𝑖Ji is a Jordan block, is called a Jordan normal form.

    Theorem: Every 𝑛 ×𝑛n×n matrix 𝐴A is similar to a Jordan normal form. Apart from the order of Jordan blocks, the Jordan normal form similar to 𝐴A is uniquely determined by 𝐴A.

    Note that in the constructed matrix 𝐵𝑖Bi, the first term is a scalar multiple of the identity, so it commutes with the second term. Therefore, the first term is the diagonalizable part of the Jordan decomposition of 𝐵𝑖Bi, and the second term is the nilpotent part.

    In the Jordan normal form of a matrix, the diagonal matrix formed by the elements on the main diagonal is the diagonalizable part of the Jordan normal form, and replacing those elements with 00 gives the nilpotent part.

    Theorem: For each Jordan block in the Jordan normal form of matrix 𝐴A:

    𝐽𝑖=⎛⎜ ⎜ ⎜ ⎜ ⎜ ⎜ ⎜ ⎜ ⎜ ⎜⎝𝜆𝑖1𝜆𝑖1⋱⋱⋱1𝜆𝑖⎞⎟ ⎟ ⎟ ⎟ ⎟ ⎟ ⎟ ⎟ ⎟ ⎟⎠Ji=(λi1λi1⋱⋱⋱1λi)

    it corresponds to an elementary divisor (𝜆−𝜆𝑖)𝑛𝑖(λ−λi)ni of the characteristic matrix 𝜆𝐼 −𝐴λI−A. All elementary divisors of 𝜆𝐼 −𝐴λI−A correspond to all Jordan blocks in the Jordan normal form of 𝐴A.

    This is because matrix 𝐴A is similar to its Jordan normal form, so their characteristic matrices are also equivalent. Transforming the characteristic matrix of the Jordan normal form to Smith normal form reveals this.

    From this theorem, with the elementary divisors of the characteristic matrix 𝜆𝐼 −𝐴λI−A, one can write the Jordan normal form of 𝐴A.

    A corollary is that matrix 𝐴A is diagonalizable if and only if all elementary divisors of 𝜆𝐼 −𝐴λI−A are linear.

    Frobenius Theorem

    Above, we noted that the Smith normal form of an 𝑛n-th order characteristic matrix has rank 𝑛n.

    Theorem: Let the Smith normal form of the characteristic matrix 𝜆𝐼 −𝐴λI−A of matrix 𝐴A be:

    diag⁡{𝑑1(𝜆),𝑑2(𝜆),⋯,𝑑𝑛(𝜆)}diag⁡{d1(λ),d2(λ),⋯,dn(λ)}

    Then the last invariant factor 𝑑𝑛(𝜆)dn(λ) is exactly the minimal polynomial 𝑚𝐴(𝜆)mA(λ) of matrix 𝐴A.

    Corollary: Equivalent conditions for matrix 𝐴A to be diagonalizable are:

    • The minimal polynomial 𝑚𝐴(𝜆)mA(λ) has no repeated roots.
    • No invariant factor of the characteristic matrix 𝜆𝐼 −𝐴λI−A has repeated roots.
    • All elementary divisors of the characteristic matrix 𝜆𝐼 −𝐴λI−A are linear.
