    Linear Maps

    Linear mappings study maps between linear spaces.

    A linear mapping can be represented in matrix form. Therefore, many concepts from matrices have corresponding relationships in linear mappings.

    Linear Mappings and Linear Transformations

    Let 𝑉V and 𝑊W be two linear spaces over field 𝐹F, and 𝑇T be a map from 𝑉V to 𝑊W.

    If for any vectors 𝑥,𝑦x,y in 𝑊W and any scalars 𝑘,𝑙k,l in field 𝐹F, we have:

    𝑇(𝑘𝑥+𝑙𝑦)=𝑘𝑇𝑥+𝑙𝑇𝑦T(kx+ly)=kTx+lTy

    Then 𝑇T is called a linear mapping from 𝑉V to 𝑊W. If 𝑊 =𝑉W=V, then 𝑇T is called a linear transformation on 𝑉V.

    For example, the identity transformation 𝑇𝑒Te keeps the space unchanged, and the zero transformation 𝑇0T0 maps the space to the zero space.

    We can denote 𝐿(𝑉,𝑊)L(V,W) as the set of all linear mappings from 𝑉V to 𝑊W. For all linear transformations 𝐿(𝑉,𝑉)L(V,V), we also write 𝐿(𝑉)L(V).

    Properties

    • A linear mapping maps the zero vector to the zero vector.
    • A linear mapping preserves linear operation forms, i.e., the linear mapping of a linear operation equals the linear operation of the mappings.
    • A linear mapping preserves linear dependence, i.e., if vectors are linearly dependent before mapping, they are also linearly dependent after mapping.

    However, a linear mapping does not preserve linear independence. Vectors linearly independent before mapping may not be linearly independent after mapping.

    Matrix Representation of Linear Mappings

    Let the dimension of 𝑉V be 𝑛n, with a basis 𝛼1,⋯,𝛼𝑛α1,⋯,αn, and the dimension of 𝑊W be 𝑚m, with a basis 𝛽1,⋯,𝛽𝑚β1,⋯,βm. Let 𝑇T be a linear mapping from 𝑉V to 𝑊W.

    Express the image of each 𝛼α under 𝑇T using 𝛽β:

    𝑇𝛼𝑗=𝑎1𝑗𝛽1+⋯+𝑎𝑚𝑗𝛽𝑚Tαj=a1jβ1+⋯+amjβm

    Using matrix notation:

    𝑇(𝛼1,⋯,𝛼𝑛)=(𝑇𝛼1,⋯,𝑇𝛼𝑛)=(𝛽1,⋯,𝛽𝑚)𝐴T(α1,⋯,αn)=(Tα1,⋯,Tαn)=(β1,⋯,βm)A

    Matrix 𝐴A is called the matrix representation of linear mapping 𝑇T with respect to these two bases.

    Kernel Space and Image Space of Linear Mappings

    The kernel space and image space here are described from the perspective of linear mappings. Through the matrix representation, we can see that the kernel space and image space of a linear mapping are consistent with those of a matrix.

    Let 𝑇T be a linear mapping from space 𝑉V to space 𝑊W. Define:

    𝑁(𝑇)={𝑥∈𝑉|𝑇𝑥=0}N(T)={x∈V|Tx=0}𝑅(𝑇)=𝐼𝑚(𝑇)={𝑦∈𝑊|𝑦=𝑇𝑥,𝑉𝑥∈𝑉}R(T)=Im(T)={y∈W|y=Tx,Vx∈V}

    It is easy to verify that 𝑁(𝑇)N(T) is a subspace of 𝑉V, and 𝑅(𝑇)R(T) is a subspace of 𝑊W. 𝑁(𝑇)N(T) and 𝑅(𝑇)R(T) are called the kernel space and image space of 𝑉V. The dimension of 𝑁(𝑇)N(T) is called the nullity or defect of 𝑇T, and the dimension of 𝑅(𝑇)R(T) is called the rank of 𝑇T.

    Theorem: Let 𝑇T be a linear mapping from space 𝑉V to space 𝑊W, with 𝑉V of finite dimension. Then 𝑁(𝑇)N(T) and 𝑅(𝑇)R(T) are both finite-dimensional, and:

    dim⁡𝑁(𝑇)+dim⁡𝑅(𝑇)=dim⁡𝑉dim⁡N(T)+dim⁡R(T)=dim⁡V

    That is, the defect plus rank of 𝑇T equals the dimension of its domain 𝑉V.

    Matrix Representation of Linear Transformations

    Let the dimension of 𝑉V be 𝑛n, with a basis 𝛼1,⋯,𝛼𝑛α1,⋯,αn. Let 𝑇T be a linear transformation on 𝑉V. Then:

    𝑇𝛼𝑗=𝑎1𝑗𝛼1+⋯+𝑎𝑛𝑗𝛼𝑛Tαj=a1jα1+⋯+anjαn

    Using matrix notation:

    𝑇(𝛼1,⋯,𝛼𝑛)=(𝑇𝛼1,⋯,𝑇𝛼𝑛)=(𝛼1,⋯,𝛼𝑛)𝐴T(α1,⋯,αn)=(Tα1,⋯,Tαn)=(α1,⋯,αn)A

    Matrix 𝐴A is called the matrix representation of linear transformation 𝑇T with respect to this basis.

    From the space structure and linearity of 𝑇T, 𝑇T is completely determined by 𝑇𝛼1,⋯,𝑇𝛼𝑛Tα1,⋯,Tαn. Hence, 𝑇T uniquely determines a matrix 𝐴A.

    Theorem: Let 𝑉V have dimension 𝑛n, and 𝛼1,⋯,𝛼𝑛α1,⋯,αn be a basis of 𝑉V. For any 𝑛 ×𝑛n×n matrix 𝐴A, there exists exactly one linear transformation 𝑇T from 𝑉V to 𝑉V such that the matrix of 𝑇T is exactly 𝐴A.

    Corollary: There is a one-to-one correspondence between 𝐿(𝑉,𝑉)L(V,V) and all 𝑛 ×𝑛n×n matrices.

    For example: the zero transformation corresponds to the zero matrix, and the identity transformation corresponds to the identity matrix.

    The Space Formed by Linear Transformations

    Theorem: 𝐿(𝑉)L(V) can also form a vector space. Define operations in 𝐿(𝑉)L(V): for any 𝑇1,𝑇2T1,T2 in 𝐿(𝑉)L(V), any 𝑥x in 𝑉V, and any 𝑘k in field 𝐹F:

    (𝑇1+𝑇2)𝑥=𝑇1𝑥+𝑇2𝑥(T1+T2)x=T1x+T2x(𝑘𝑇1)𝑥=𝑘(𝑇1𝑥)(kT1)x=k(T1x)

    It is easy to verify that 𝐿(𝑉)L(V) is a vector space over 𝐹F, called the linear transformation space.

    For linear transformations 𝑇1T1 and 𝑇2T2 in 𝐿(𝑉)L(V), define the product 𝑇1𝑇2T1T2 as:

    (𝑇1𝑇2)𝑥=𝑇2(𝑇1𝑥)(T1T2)x=T2(T1x)

    It can be verified that (𝑇1𝑇2)(T1T2) is also a linear transformation in 𝐿(𝑉)L(V). The product of linear transformations satisfies the associative law but not the commutative law, similar to matrix multiplication.

    For linear transformation 𝑇1T1 in 𝐿(𝑉)L(V), if there exists linear transformation 𝑇2T2 in 𝐿(𝑉)L(V) such that for any vector 𝑥x in 𝑉V:

    (𝑇1𝑇2)𝑥=𝑇1(𝑇2𝑥)=𝑥(T1T2)x=T1(T2x)=x

    Then 𝑇2T2 is called the inverse of 𝑇1T1, denoted:

    𝑇2=𝑇−11T2=T1−1

    And:

    𝑇1𝑇2=𝑇2𝑇1=𝑇𝑒T1T2=T2T1=Te

    Theorem: Let 𝑉V have dimension 𝑛n, and 𝛼1,⋯,𝛼𝑛α1,⋯,αn be a basis of 𝑉V. Under this basis, the matrix of linear transformation 𝑇1T1 is 𝐴A, and the matrix of 𝑇2T2 is 𝐵B. Then:

    • The matrix of linear transformation 𝑇1 +𝑇2T1+T2 is 𝐴 +𝐵A+B
    • The matrix of scalar multiplication 𝑘𝑇1kT1 is 𝑘𝐴kA
    • The matrix of product 𝑇1𝑇2T1T2 is 𝐴𝐵AB
    • If the inverse of linear transformation 𝑇1T1 exists, its matrix is 𝐴−1A−1

    Coordinates

    Let the 𝑛n vectors 𝑥x be a basis of 𝑛n-dimensional space 𝑉V. For any vector 𝑦y in 𝑉V, let:

    𝑦=𝑎1𝑥1+𝑎2𝑥2+⋯+𝑎𝑛𝑥𝑛=(𝑥1,𝑥2,⋯,𝑥𝑛)⎛⎜ ⎜ ⎜ ⎜ ⎜ ⎜⎝𝑎1𝑎2⋮𝑎𝑛⎞⎟ ⎟ ⎟ ⎟ ⎟ ⎟⎠y=a1x1+a2x2+⋯+anxn=(x1,x2,⋯,xn)(a1a2⋮an)

    The column vector:

    ⎛⎜ ⎜ ⎜ ⎜ ⎜ ⎜⎝𝑎1𝑎2⋮𝑎𝑛⎞⎟ ⎟ ⎟ ⎟ ⎟ ⎟⎠(a1a2⋮an)

    is called the coordinate of vector 𝑦y with respect to basis 𝑥1,𝑥2,⋯,𝑥𝑛x1,x2,⋯,xn.

    As you can see, coordinates are column vectors composed of scalars from the field, which should be distinguished from vectors in the Abelian group.

    Coordinate Transformation Formula

    Let 𝑉V have dimension 𝑛n. Let 𝑇T be a transformation in 𝐿(𝑉)L(V), with matrix 𝐴A under basis 𝛼1,⋯,𝛼𝑛α1,⋯,αn. Let:

    𝜉=(𝛼1,⋯,𝛼𝑛)⎛⎜ ⎜ ⎜ ⎜ ⎜ ⎜⎝𝑥1𝑥2⋮𝑥𝑛⎞⎟ ⎟ ⎟ ⎟ ⎟ ⎟⎠ξ=(α1,⋯,αn)(x1x2⋮xn)

    And:

    𝑇𝜉=𝑇(𝛼1,⋯,𝛼𝑛)⎛⎜ ⎜ ⎜ ⎜ ⎜ ⎜⎝𝑦1𝑦2⋮𝑦𝑛⎞⎟ ⎟ ⎟ ⎟ ⎟ ⎟⎠Tξ=T(α1,⋯,αn)(y1y2⋮yn)

    Then:

    𝑇𝜉=𝑇(𝛼1,⋯,𝛼𝑛)⎛⎜ ⎜ ⎜ ⎜ ⎜ ⎜⎝𝑦1𝑦2⋮𝑦𝑛⎞⎟ ⎟ ⎟ ⎟ ⎟ ⎟⎠=(𝛼1,⋯,𝛼𝑛)𝐴⎛⎜ ⎜ ⎜ ⎜ ⎜ ⎜⎝𝑥1𝑥2⋮𝑥𝑛⎞⎟ ⎟ ⎟ ⎟ ⎟ ⎟⎠Tξ=T(α1,⋯,αn)(y1y2⋮yn)=(α1,⋯,αn)A(x1x2⋮xn)

    Column vectors in space 𝑉V are essentially all in the form "basis times coordinate". A column vector 𝑥x in space 𝑉V itself uses identity matrix 𝐼I as the basis, i.e., 𝑥 =𝐼𝑥x=Ix.

    Only when the same basis is fixed, and we consider a pure linear transformation 𝑇T, the coordinate is simply left-multiplied by a regular matrix.

    Consider linear transformation 𝑇T as a "filter" observing space 𝑉V. The action of 𝑇T is on space 𝑉V, distorting it. After applying the filter, the point itself doesn't change position.

    This theorem also shows that for a linear transformation 𝑇T on a column vector basis, it is equivalent to right-multiplying the basis by a transition matrix.

    Therefore, between different bases, coordinates are related by left-multiplying by the inverse of the transition matrix.

    Transition Matrix

    Let the 𝑛n vectors 𝑥x and 𝑛n vectors 𝑦y be two bases of space 𝑉V. For 1 ≤𝑖 ≤𝑛1≤i≤n, let each vector 𝑦𝑖yi have coordinate:

    𝑦𝑖=(𝑥1,𝑥2,⋯,𝑥𝑛)⎛⎜ ⎜ ⎜ ⎜ ⎜ ⎜⎝𝑎1𝑖𝑎2𝑖⋮𝑎𝑛𝑖⎞⎟ ⎟ ⎟ ⎟ ⎟ ⎟⎠yi=(x1,x2,⋯,xn)(a1ia2i⋮ani)

    Then the 𝑛n vectors 𝑦y form the matrix on the left side of the equation, and the 𝑛n coordinates form the matrix 𝐴A on the right side:

    (𝑦1,𝑦2,⋯,𝑦𝑛)=(𝑥1,𝑥2,⋯,𝑥𝑛)𝐴(y1,y2,⋯,yn)=(x1,x2,⋯,xn)A

    Matrix 𝐴A is called the transition matrix from basis 𝑥1,𝑥2⋯,𝑥𝑛x1,x2⋯,xn to basis 𝑦1,𝑦2⋯,𝑦𝑛y1,y2⋯,yn, also called the change of basis matrix.

    Clearly, the transition matrix is invertible. From the above, the transition matrix from basis 𝑦1,𝑦2⋯,𝑦𝑛y1,y2⋯,yn to basis 𝑥1,𝑥2⋯,𝑥𝑛x1,x2⋯,xn is 𝐴−1A−1.

    As noted earlier, the transition matrix is composed of scalars from the field, not vectors from the Abelian group arranged as a matrix. They should be distinguished.

    Let the 𝑛n vectors 𝑥x and 𝑛n vectors 𝑦y be two bases of space 𝑉V. For the same vector 𝑧z in space 𝑉V, we have:

    𝑧=(𝑥1,𝑥2,⋯,𝑥𝑛)⎛⎜ ⎜ ⎜ ⎜ ⎜ ⎜⎝𝜉1𝜉2⋮𝜉𝑛⎞⎟ ⎟ ⎟ ⎟ ⎟ ⎟⎠=(𝑦1,𝑦2⋯,𝑦𝑛)⎛⎜ ⎜ ⎜ ⎜ ⎜ ⎜⎝𝜂1𝜂2⋮𝜂𝑛⎞⎟ ⎟ ⎟ ⎟ ⎟ ⎟⎠z=(x1,x2,⋯,xn)(ξ1ξ2⋮ξn)=(y1,y2⋯,yn)(η1η2⋮ηn)

    Substituting from earlier:

    (𝑦1,𝑦2⋯,𝑦𝑛)=(𝑥1,𝑥2⋯,𝑥𝑛)𝐴(y1,y2⋯,yn)=(x1,x2⋯,xn)A

    By uniqueness, we get:

    ⎛⎜ ⎜ ⎜ ⎜ ⎜ ⎜⎝𝜉1𝜉2⋮𝜉𝑛⎞⎟ ⎟ ⎟ ⎟ ⎟ ⎟⎠=𝐴⎛⎜ ⎜ ⎜ ⎜ ⎜ ⎜⎝𝜂1𝜂2⋮𝜂𝑛⎞⎟ ⎟ ⎟ ⎟ ⎟ ⎟⎠(ξ1ξ2⋮ξn)=A(η1η2⋮ηn)

    Or:

    ⎛⎜ ⎜ ⎜ ⎜ ⎜ ⎜⎝𝜂1𝜂2⋮𝜂𝑛⎞⎟ ⎟ ⎟ ⎟ ⎟ ⎟⎠=𝐴−1⎛⎜ ⎜ ⎜ ⎜ ⎜ ⎜⎝𝜉1𝜉2⋮𝜉𝑛⎞⎟ ⎟ ⎟ ⎟ ⎟ ⎟⎠(η1η2⋮ηn)=A−1(ξ1ξ2⋮ξn)

    This is purely a transformation between coordinates. All coordinate transformation formulas are in the scalar field. As distinguished earlier, vectors in linear spaces and Abelian groups are "abstract vectors", while coordinates and elements of transition matrices are in the scalar field, considered as "concrete vectors". The two types should be considered "different things".

    A matrix can transform the entire space, i.e., all coordinates. Column vector 𝑥x as a coordinate ranges over the entire space.

    Identity matrix 𝐼I is composed of unit vectors. Matrix 𝐴A transforms identity matrix 𝐼I to each column vector of matrix 𝐴A, i.e., transforms unit vectors to each column vector of matrix 𝐴A. Therefore, left-multiplying by matrix 𝐴A can also be seen as performing such a transformation on the space.

    Left-multiplying a vector by a matrix can also be seen as left-multiplying coordinates by a vector set. Using the coordinate perspective:

    𝐼𝑦=𝑋𝑎Iy=Xa

    The same column vector 𝑦y, in the "normal" space with identity matrix 𝐼I as the basis, has coordinate 𝑦y, but in the new transformed space, the coordinate will be recorded as 𝑎a. In this way, matrix 𝑋X is not only a basis in the normal space but also the transition matrix from vector set 𝐼I to vector set 𝑋X.

    Linear transformation 𝑇T maps one basis to another, so coordinates are also mapped to other coordinates.

    If the linear transformation 𝑇T that maps basis 𝛼α to 𝛽β has transition matrix 𝐴A, then the corresponding basis matrix satisfies 𝛽 =𝛼𝐴β=αA.

    Thus, the coordinate relationship is exactly reversed. Suppose after linear transformation 𝑇T, the coordinate is 𝑏b, i.e., after applying the filter, we observe coordinate 𝑏b. Then the point's representation in 𝑉V is 𝛽𝑏βb. The way to restore it is using the transition matrix, writing the point's representation in 𝑉V as 𝛼𝐴𝑏αAb. Then the coordinate transformation by left-multiplying by the inverse of the transition matrix is now clear.

    Linear Transformations and Matrix Similarity

    Consider a linear transformation 𝑇T on space 𝑉V with respect to basis 𝛼α of 𝑉V:

    Linear transformation 𝑇T acts on basis 𝛼α, mapping 𝛼α to 𝑇(𝛼)T(α), which is equivalent to right-multiplying 𝛼α by 𝐴A, i.e., 𝑇(𝛼) =𝛼𝐴T(α)=αA.

    Matrix similarity considers the question: same linear transformation 𝑇T, described as matrix 𝐵B in space 𝑉V with basis 𝛽β, and as matrix 𝐴A in space 𝑉V with basis 𝛼α.

    If the transition matrix is 𝐶C, i.e., 𝛽 =𝛼𝐶β=αC, what is the relationship between the two descriptions 𝐵B and 𝐴A?

    Since it is the same transformation 𝑇T, we find that the relationship of the transition matrix always holds before and after transformation:

    𝑇(𝛽)=𝑇(𝛼)𝐶=𝛼𝐴𝐶T(β)=T(α)C=αAC

    Linear transformation 𝑇T from the perspective of basis 𝛽β is still right-multiplication. Basis 𝛽β transforms to basis 𝛼α then right-multiplies by 𝐶C. Before and after transformation, the relationship of transition matrix 𝐶C is maintained:

    𝑇(𝛽)=𝛽𝐵=𝛼𝐶𝐵T(β)=βB=αCB

    Thus the problem is solved:

    𝐵=𝐶−1𝐴𝐶B=C−1AC

    Theorem: Let 𝑇T be a transformation in 𝐿(𝑉)L(V). Then 𝑇T is similar under different bases.

    For square matrices 𝐴A and 𝐵B, if there exists an invertible matrix 𝐶C such that 𝐵 =𝐶−1𝐴𝐶B=C−1AC, then 𝐴A and 𝐵B are similar.

    Matrix similarity preserves rank, so matrix similarity implies matrix equivalence. However, two equivalent matrices are not necessarily similar.

    Since matrix similarity is closely related to shape, there is no relationship between matrix similarity and vector set equivalence or system equivalence.

    Looking back, the interpretation of matrix similarity involves four equations: 𝛽 =𝛼𝐶β=αC, 𝑇(𝛼) =𝛼𝐴T(α)=αA, 𝑇(𝛽) =𝛽𝐵T(β)=βB, 𝑇(𝛽) =𝑇(𝛼)𝐶T(β)=T(α)C.
