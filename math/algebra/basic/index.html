    Basic Concepts

    This chapter provides a brief introduction to abstract algebra. Currently, the main content of competitive programming contests does not directly test abstract algebra knowledge; however, basic concepts of abstract functions often appear in algorithm descriptions or problem solutions, enabling readers with a foundation in abstract algebra to understand certain algorithms more quickly. Therefore, this chapter is not mandatory for any competitor, but serves as reference material for interested readers or those who may benefit from it. Additionally, this chapter will avoid overly comprehensive or deep coverage of abstract algebra1, focusing instead on fundamental concepts and parts most closely related to other OI topics. Readers wishing to systematically study abstract algebra should consult specialized textbooks.

    To better help readers understand the potential benefits of studying this material, here are some examples of how abstract algebra may appear in competitive programming:

    • Many theorems in number theory and polynomial theory are special cases of results in abstract algebra;
    • In data structures, structures like segment trees can maintain information of monoids, and many DP recurrence relations can be abstracted into such monoid structures;
    • In combinatorics, the rigorous statement and proof of Pólya's enumeration theorem requires concepts from group theory.

    Based on this, this chapter will focus on essential foundational knowledge and parts directly related to these applications. As a starting point, this article introduces the basic concepts of groups, rings, and fields.

    Groups

    A group is defined as follows.

    Group

    Let 𝐺G be a nonempty set with a binary operation ⋅ :𝐺 ×𝐺 →𝐺⋅:G×G→G. If they satisfy the following properties, then (𝐺, ⋅)(G,⋅) is called a group:

    1. Associative property: For all 𝑎,𝑏,𝑐 ∈𝐺a,b,c∈G, we have 𝑎 ⋅(𝑏 ⋅𝑐) =(𝑎 ⋅𝑏) ⋅𝑐a⋅(b⋅c)=(a⋅b)⋅c;
    2. Existence of identity element: There exists 𝑒 ∈𝐺e∈G such that for any 𝑎 ∈𝐺a∈G, we have 𝑎 ⋅𝑒 =𝑒 ⋅𝑎 =𝑎a⋅e=e⋅a=a. Here, 𝑒e is called the identity element (or identity) of 𝐺G;
    3. Existence of inverse element: For all 𝑎 ∈𝐺a∈G, there exists 𝑏 ∈𝐺b∈G such that 𝑎 ⋅𝑏 =𝑏 ⋅𝑎 =𝑒a⋅b=b⋅a=e. Here, 𝑏b is called the inverse element of 𝑎a.
    On the closure condition in the definition

    The binary operation here implicitly includes the so-called closure condition, i.e., for any 𝑎,𝑏 ∈𝐺a,b∈G, we have 𝑎 ⋅𝑏 ∈𝐺a⋅b∈G. Some texts may list this separately.

    Basic properties of groups

    For a group (𝐺, ⋅)(G,⋅), the following properties always hold:

    1. For any finite sequence {𝑔𝑖}𝑘𝑖=1 ⊆𝐺{gi}i=1k⊆G, the product 𝑔1 ⋅𝑔2 ⋅⋯ ⋅𝑔𝑘g1⋅g2⋅⋯⋅gk is independent of how parentheses are placed;
    2. The identity element 𝑒e is always unique;
    3. For any element 𝑎 ∈𝐺a∈G, its inverse 𝑎−1a−1 is also unique;
    4. Cancellation law: For 𝑎,𝑏,𝑐 ∈𝐺a,b,c∈G, if 𝑎 ⋅𝑐 =𝑏 ⋅𝑐a⋅c=b⋅c or 𝑐 ⋅𝑎 =𝑐 ⋅𝑏c⋅a=c⋅b, then 𝑎 =𝑏a=b.

    Groups are quite common. Intuitively, all structure-preserving transformations automatically form a group. Here are some common types of groups.

    Examples of groups
    • Symmetric group: All permutations on a set 𝑀M, i.e., bijections from 𝑀M to itself, form a group 𝑆𝑀SM under composition of mappings. The identity element is the identity transformation, and the inverse is the inverse mapping (a bijection necessarily has an inverse). If the set 𝑀M is finite with size 𝑛n, it is often denoted 𝑆𝑛Sn, called the symmetric group of degree 𝑛n.
    • Symmetry group: For a geometric figure, all transformations that map it onto itself form a group under composition. This describes the spatial symmetry of the geometric figure. See Common spatial symmetry groups for specific examples.
    • Additive group of integers: The set of integers 𝐙Z forms a group (𝐙, +)(Z,+) under addition. The identity element is 00, and the inverse is the additive inverse.
    • Multiplicative group of integers modulo 𝑛n: For a modulus 𝑛n, all integers coprime to 𝑛n, represented by their congruence classes, form a group ((𝐙/𝑛𝐙)×, ×)((Z/nZ)×,×) under multiplication. The identity element is ¯11¯, and the inverse is the multiplicative inverse modulo 𝑛n (the corresponding congruence class), whose existence is guaranteed by the Bézout's theorem. See Multiplicative group of integers modulo 𝑛n for detailed structure analysis.
    • General linear group: All invertible 𝑛 ×𝑛n×n matrices over a number field 𝐹F form a group 𝐺𝐿𝑛(𝐹)GLn(F) under matrix multiplication. The identity element is the identity matrix, and the inverse is the inverse matrix.

    To better understand the definition of groups, it helps to see some examples that are not groups.

    Examples that are not groups
    • All mappings from 𝑀M to itself (not necessarily bijections) do not form a group, because non-bijective mappings do not have inverses.
    • Integers under multiplication do not form a group, because 22 has no multiplicative inverse in the integers.
    • Positive integers under addition do not form a group, because positive integers have no additive identity.
    • All nonzero congruence classes modulo 𝑛n often do not form a group under multiplication. For example, in (𝐙/6𝐙) ∖{――0}(Z/6Z)∖{0―}, we have ――2 ×――3 =――02―×3―=0―, which is not in this set, meaning multiplication is not a well-defined binary operation on this set (i.e., it does not satisfy closure).

    Sometimes, it is necessary to discuss properties of these less complete structures. Therefore, the following concepts can be defined, which are more general than groups.

    Semigroup

    For a nonempty set 𝐺G with a binary operation ⋅⋅, if the operation satisfies the associative property, then (𝐺, ⋅)(G,⋅) is called a semigroup.

    Monoid

    For a semigroup (𝐺, ⋅)(G,⋅), if it also has an identity element, then (𝐺, ⋅)(G,⋅) is called a monoid.

    Examples of monoids and semigroups

    In the above examples, (𝐍+, +)(N+,+) is a semigroup, while (𝐙, ×)(Z,×) is a monoid.

    Finally, many familiar operations on groups satisfy not only the associative property but also the commutative property. Such groups have relatively simple structure and are called Abelian groups, or commutative groups.

    Abelian group

    For a group (𝐺, ⋅)(G,⋅), if the operation ⋅⋅ also satisfies the commutative property, i.e., for all 𝑎,𝑏 ∈𝐺a,b∈G, we have 𝑎 ⋅𝑏 =𝑏 ⋅𝑎a⋅b=b⋅a, then (𝐺, ⋅)(G,⋅) is called an Abelian group or commutative group.

    Examples of Abelian and non-Abelian groups
    • The integer additive group (𝐙, +)(Z,+) is an Abelian group.
    • When 𝑛 ≥3n≥3, the symmetric group 𝑆𝑛Sn is not an Abelian group.

    These are the basic definitions related to group theory. For more on group theory, see Group Theory or related books.

    Rings

    A ring is defined as follows.

    Ring

    For a nonempty set 𝑅R with two binary operations + :𝑅 ×𝑅 →𝑅+:R×R→R and ⋅ :𝑅 ×𝑅 →𝑅⋅:R×R→R, if they satisfy the following properties, then (𝑅, +, ⋅)(R,+,⋅) is a ring:

    1. (𝑅, +)(R,+) forms an Abelian group, with identity denoted 00, and the additive inverse of 𝑎 ∈𝑅a∈R denoted −𝑎−a.
    2. (𝑅, ⋅)(R,⋅) forms a semigroup, i.e., ⋅⋅ satisfies the associative property.
    3. Distributive property: For all 𝑎,𝑏,𝑐 ∈𝑅a,b,c∈R, we have 𝑎 ⋅(𝑏 +𝑐) =𝑎 ⋅𝑏 +𝑎 ⋅𝑐a⋅(b+c)=a⋅b+a⋅c and (𝑎 +𝑏) ⋅𝑐 =𝑎 ⋅𝑐 +𝑏 ⋅𝑐(a+b)⋅c=a⋅c+b⋅c.

    For convenience, these two binary operations ++ and ⋅⋅ are often called addition and multiplication of the ring, respectively. Correspondingly, the additive identity is called the zero, and the multiplicative identity (if it exists) is called the identity. Care should be taken to avoid confusion with addition and multiplication in specific number systems, as well as the natural number zero and one.

    On whether the definition requires a multiplicative identity

    In some definitions, a ring must have a multiplicative identity; correspondingly, rings without a multiplicative identity are called rngs or pseudo-rings. Context should determine which definition is being used. Wikipedia uses this definition3.

    The additive structure of rings is quite simple, but the multiplicative structure is very primitive. Therefore, analogous to groups, if we impose additional requirements on multiplication, we can obtain the following related definitions.

    Ring with identity

    For a ring (𝑅, +, ⋅)(R,+,⋅), if it has an identity, i.e., there exists a multiplicative identity denoted 11, then (𝑅, +, ⋅)(R,+,⋅) is called a ring with identity.

    Division ring

    For a nonzero ring with identity (𝑅, +, ⋅)(R,+,⋅), if for all nonzero elements 𝑎 ∈𝑅a∈R, there exists a multiplicative inverse (denoted 𝑎−1a−1), then (𝑅, +, ⋅)(R,+,⋅) is called a division ring.

    Commutative ring

    For a ring (𝑅, +, ⋅)(R,+,⋅), if its multiplication satisfies the commutative property, then (𝑅, +, ⋅)(R,+,⋅) is called a commutative ring.

    An interesting point in the definition of division rings is that it treats 00 as a special element in the multiplicative structure. This is because 0 =0 ⋅𝑎 =𝑎 ⋅00=0⋅a=a⋅02. That is, in a ring, the additive identity multiplied by any element yields itself. Thus, it naturally cannot have a multiplicative inverse unless it itself is the multiplicative identity. The only such ring is the zero ring (see the example below).

    The insight here is that to understand the multiplicative structure of a general ring, one should remove the influence of the additive identity and consider 𝑅 ∖{0}R∖{0}. Based on this idea, we have the following definitions.

    Zero divisor

    For a ring (𝑅, +, ⋅)(R,+,⋅), if there exists 𝑏 ∈𝑅b∈R with 𝑏 ≠0b≠0 such that 𝑎 ⋅𝑏 =0a⋅b=0 or 𝑏 ⋅𝑎 =0b⋅a=0, then the nonzero element 𝑎a is called a zero divisor.

    Unit

    For a ring (𝑅, +, ⋅)(R,+,⋅), if an element 𝑎a has a multiplicative inverse, i.e., there exists 𝑏 ∈𝑅b∈R such that 𝑎 ⋅𝑏 =𝑏 ⋅𝑎 =1a⋅b=b⋅a=1, then the element 𝑎 ∈𝑅a∈R is called a unit.

    "Unit" vs "Identity"

    Please do not confuse these two concepts. To avoid confusion, this part of abstract algebra will use the term "invertible element" instead of "unit".

    Zero divisors cannot be invertible, and invertible elements cannot be zero divisors. However, a nonzero element can be neither a zero divisor nor an invertible element.

    If a ring has no zero divisors, then the set of all nonzero elements is closed under multiplication, i.e., (𝑅 ∖{0}, ⋅)(R∖{0},⋅) forms a semigroup. Furthermore, if we require it to be a commutative monoid, we get the definition of an integral domain.

    Integral domain

    For a nonzero ring (𝑅, +, ⋅)(R,+,⋅), if it is a commutative ring, has a multiplicative identity, and has no zero divisors, then it is called an integral domain.

    Although elements in an integral domain do not necessarily have inverses, the property of having no zero divisors is sufficient to establish a cancellation law on integral domains.

    Cancellation law in integral domains

    Let 𝑅R be an integral domain with elements 𝑎,𝑏,𝑐 ∈𝑅a,b,c∈R and 𝑎 ≠0a≠0. If 𝑎𝑏 =𝑎𝑐ab=ac, then necessarily 𝑏 =𝑐b=c.

    For a general ring with identity, if we consider only its invertible elements, we again obtain a group structure. This is called the multiplicative group or the group of units.

    Multiplicative group (group of units)

    For a ring with identity (𝑅, +, ⋅)(R,+,⋅), let 𝑅×R× be the set of all invertible elements in 𝑅R. Then (𝑅×, ⋅)(R×,⋅) forms a group, called the multiplicative group or unit group of the ring 𝑅R.

    Some of the simplest examples of rings are as follows.

    Examples of rings
    • Zero ring: The set {0}{0} under ordinary addition ++ and multiplication ×× forms a ring, called the zero ring. It is the only ring with one element, and the only ring where the additive and multiplicative identities coincide.
    • Integer ring: The set of integers 𝐙Z with the ordinary addition ++ and multiplication ×× forms the ring (𝐙, +, ×)(Z,+,×). Actually, this is an integral domain, but it is not a division ring.
    • Polynomial ring: For a ring 𝑅R, one can define a polynomial ring 𝑅[𝑥]R[x]. If 𝑅R is an integral domain, then this polynomial ring is necessarily an integral domain.

    Fields

    A field is an algebraic structure with stronger properties than a ring. Specifically, a field is a commutative division ring. Its complete definition can also be written.

    Field

    For a nonempty set 𝐹F with two binary operations + :𝐹 ×𝐹 →𝐹+:F×F→F and ⋅ :𝐹 ×𝐹 →𝐹⋅:F×F→F, if they satisfy the following properties, then (𝐹, +, ⋅)(F,+,⋅) is a field:

    1. (𝐹, +)(F,+) forms an Abelian group, with identity denoted 00, and the additive inverse of 𝑎 ∈𝐹a∈F denoted −𝑎−a.
    2. (𝐹 ∖{0}, ⋅)(F∖{0},⋅) forms an Abelian group, with identity denoted 11, and the multiplicative inverse of 𝑎 ∈𝐹 ∖{0}a∈F∖{0} denoted 𝑎−1a−1.

    In other words, a field is an algebraic structure closed under the four arithmetic operations: addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division.

    Common examples of fields are as follows.

    Examples of fields
    • Number fields: The rational number set 𝐐Q, real number set 𝐑R, and complex number set 𝐂C all form fields under ordinary addition and multiplication.
    • Finite fields: The set of integer congruence classes modulo a prime 𝑝p, i.e., 𝐙/𝑝𝐙Z/pZ, forms a field under addition and multiplication of congruence classes. There are also other finite fields besides these; their structure is uniquely determined by their size, which must be a prime power.

    • Fraction field: Let (𝑅, +, ⋅)(R,+,⋅) be an integral domain. Consider the set 𝑄Q of elements of the form 𝑎𝑏−1ab−1. Strictly speaking, define an equivalence relation on the set 𝑅 ×(𝑅 ∖{0})R×(R∖{0}): (𝑎1,𝑏1) ∼(𝑎2,𝑏2)(a1,b1)∼(a2,b2) if and only if 𝑎1𝑏2 =𝑎2𝑏1a1b2=a2b1.       𝑎1𝑏−11+𝑎2𝑏−12=(𝑎1⋅𝑏2+𝑎2⋅𝑏1)(𝑏1⋅𝑏2)−1,(𝑎1𝑏−11)⋅(𝑎2𝑏−12)=(𝑎1⋅𝑎2)(𝑏1⋅𝑏2)−1a1b1−1+a2b2−1=(a1⋅b2+a2⋅b1)(b1⋅b2)−1,(a1b1−1)⋅(a2b2−1)=(a1⋅a2)(b1⋅b2)−1

    • Quadratic field: This is obtained by adjoining √𝑑d to the rational field 𝐐Q, where 𝑑 ≠0,1d≠0,1 is square-free. Related content can be found in Quadratic fields.

    Compared to rings, fields have very simple additive and multiplicative structures. Therefore, the structure of fields themselves is often quite simple. This makes the study of fields quite different from the study of rings; typically, one studies field extensions and corresponding Galois theory. In competitive programming, sometimes calculations need to be performed on extensions of the rational field or finite fields. For content related to field theory, see Field Theory or related books.
