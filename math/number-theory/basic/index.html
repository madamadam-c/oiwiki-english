    Multiples and Divisors

    Definition

    If 𝑎 ∣𝑏a∣b, then 𝑏b is a multiple of 𝑎a, and 𝑎a is a divisor of 𝑏b.

    00 is a multiple of all non-zero integers. For an integer 𝑏 ≠0b≠0, 𝑏b has only finitely many divisors.

    Trivial divisors (trivial factors): For an integer 𝑏 ≠0b≠0, ±1±1 and ±𝑏±b are the trivial divisors of 𝑏b. When 𝑏 = ±1b=±1, 𝑏b has only two trivial divisors.

    Other divisors of an integer 𝑏 ≠0b≠0 are called proper divisors (proper factors, non-trivial divisors, non-trivial factors).

    Properties of divisors:

    • Let 𝑏 ≠0b≠0 be an integer. When 𝑑d ranges over all divisors of 𝑏b, 𝑏𝑑bd also ranges over all divisors of 𝑏b.
    • Let 𝑏 >0b>0 be an integer. When 𝑑d ranges over all positive divisors of 𝑏b, 𝑏𝑑bd also ranges over all positive divisors of 𝑏b.

    In specific problems, unless otherwise specified, divisors always refer to positive divisors.

    Division with Remainder

    There are two other common ways to define the remainder:

    In division with remainder, the remainder is only the least non-negative remainder. Unless otherwise specified, the remainder always refers to the least non-negative remainder.

    Properties of remainders:

    • When any integer is divided by a positive integer 𝑎a, the remainder is exactly one of the 𝑎a numbers from 00 to (𝑎 −1)(a−1).
    • After dividing 𝑎a consecutive integers by the positive integer 𝑎a, they cover exactly these 𝑎a remainders. In particular, there is exactly one number divisible by 𝑎a.

    Prime Numbers and Composite Numbers

    For algorithms related to prime numbers, see Prime Numbers.

    Definition

    Let 𝑝 ≠0, ±1p≠0,±1 be an integer. If 𝑝p has no divisors other than the trivial ones, then 𝑝p is called a prime number (irreducible number).

    If 𝑎 ≠0, ±1a≠0,±1 is an integer and 𝑎a is not a prime number, then 𝑎a is called a composite number.

    𝑝p and −𝑝−p are always both primes or both composites. Unless otherwise specified, prime numbers always refer to positive prime numbers.

    If a factor of an integer is a prime number, that prime number is called a prime factor (prime divisor) of that integer.

    Simple properties of prime and composite numbers:

    • An integer 𝑎 >1a>1 is composite if and only if 𝑎a can be expressed as the product of integers 𝑑d and 𝑒e (1 <𝑑,𝑒 <𝑎1<d,e<a).
    • If a prime 𝑝p has a divisor 𝑑 >1d>1, then 𝑑 =𝑝d=p.
    • Any integer 𝑎 >1a>1 can be expressed as a product of prime numbers.
    • For a composite number 𝑎a, there must exist a prime number 𝑝 ≤√𝑎p≤a such that 𝑝 ∣𝑎p∣a.
    • There are infinitely many prime numbers.
    • All prime numbers greater than 33 can be expressed in the form 6𝑛 ±16n±12.

    Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic

    Fundamental Lemma of Arithmetic

    Let 𝑝p be a prime number, 𝑝 ∣𝑎1𝑎2p∣a1a2. Then at least one of 𝑝 ∣𝑎1p∣a1 and 𝑝 ∣𝑎2p∣a2 holds.

    The motivation for this definition can be found in Prime Ideals.

    Fundamental Theorem of Arithmetic (Unique Factorization Theorem)

    For a positive integer 𝑎a, there must exist a representation:

    𝑎=𝑝1𝑝2⋯𝑝𝑠a=p1p2⋯ps

    where 𝑝𝑗(1 ≤𝑗 ≤𝑠)pj(1≤j≤s) are prime numbers. Moreover, this representation is unique up to ordering.

    Combining identical prime numbers in the above representation, we get:

    𝑎=𝑝1𝛼1𝑝2𝛼2⋯𝑝𝑠𝛼𝑠,𝑝1<𝑝2<⋯<𝑝𝑠a=p1α1p2α2⋯psαs,p1<p2<⋯<ps

    The fundamental theorem of arithmetic and the fundamental lemma of arithmetic are equivalent.
