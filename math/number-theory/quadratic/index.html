    Quadratic Fields

    This article provides a brief introduction to the concepts related to quadratic fields. Two important examples are Gaussian integers and Eisenstein integers, which can be used to solve some number theory problems.

    Basic Concepts

    This section first introduces a series of basic concepts. Quadratic fields and quadratic integer rings are both special cases of the more general concepts of algebraic extension fields and algebraic integer rings, so almost all definitions and conclusions in this section can be appropriately generalized to the general case. The discussion in this section will be limited to the special case of quadratic fields without introducing too much algebraic number theory content.

    Quadratic Fields

    Elements of quadratic fields are all quadratic algebraic numbers.

    A quadratic algebraic number is a complex number that can be expressed as a solution of a univariate quadratic equation with integer coefficients. By the quadratic formula, all quadratic algebraic numbers can be expressed in the form

    𝑎+𝑏√𝑑a+bd

    where 𝑎a and 𝑏b are rational numbers, and 𝑑d is a squarefree integer. Any number of this form is also a quadratic algebraic number. Quadratic algebraic numbers can be divided into rational numbers and quadratic irrational numbers. Clearly, a quadratic irrational number has a unique representation in the above form.

    For any squarefree integer 𝑑 ≠0,1d≠0,1, one can verify that the set 𝑄(√𝑑) ={𝑎 +𝑏√𝑑 :𝑎,𝑏 ∈𝐐}Q(d)={a+bd:a,b∈Q} is closed under addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division. Since a set closed under the four arithmetic operations is called a field, the set 𝑄(√𝑑)Q(d) is called a quadratic field. Every quadratic field contains all rational numbers, so they are all quadratic extensions of the rational number field 𝐐Q. When 𝑑 >0d>0, all numbers in the quadratic field 𝐐(√𝑑)Q(d) are real numbers, and 𝐐(√𝑑)Q(d) is called a real quadratic field; when 𝑑 <0d<0, all numbers in the quadratic field except rational numbers are complex numbers, and 𝐐(√𝑑)Q(d) is called an imaginary quadratic field.

    Conjugate and Norm

    The conjugate of the quadratic irrational number 𝑎 +𝑏√𝑑a+bd is defined as 𝑎 −𝑏√𝑑a−bd. Conjugate quadratic irrational numbers are the two distinct roots of the same integer-coefficient quadratic equation. In real quadratic fields, the conjugate of a quadratic irrational number does not coincide with its complex conjugate; in imaginary quadratic fields, the conjugate of a quadratic irrational number is its complex conjugate. Finally, the conjugate of a rational number is defined as itself. This defines the concept of conjugate for all quadratic algebraic numbers.

    In any quadratic field, equations generated by the four arithmetic operations of addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division cannot distinguish between conjugates. That is, in any equation, replacing each number with its conjugate, or equivalently changing the sign inside each square root, the equation remains true.

    Using conjugates, several mappings from quadratic algebraic numbers to rational numbers can be constructed, which can transform discussions about quadratic algebraic numbers into discussions about rational numbers. The simpler one is the trace of a quadratic algebraic number 𝛼α, which is the sum of a number and its conjugate, denoted tr⁡(𝛼)tr⁡(α). Since this is actually twice its rational part, it does not provide much information.

    More useful is the norm of a quadratic algebraic number. The product of a quadratic algebraic number and its conjugate is called its norm:

    𝑁(𝑎+𝑏√𝑑)=𝑎2−𝑑𝑏2N(a+bd)=a2−db2

    Obviously, in an imaginary quadratic field, the concept of norm coincides with the concept of the square of the absolute value of a complex number; but in a real quadratic field, these two concepts do not coincide.

    The norm has good properties. First, since 𝑑d is squarefree and cannot be a perfect square, only 00 has norm 00. Second, the norm preserves multiplication and division:

    𝑁(𝑎1+𝑏1√𝑑)𝑁(𝑎2+𝑏2√𝑑)=𝑁((𝑎1+𝑏1√𝑑)(𝑎2+𝑏2√𝑑))N(a1+b1d)N(a2+b2d)=N((a1+b1d)(a2+b2d))𝑁(𝑎1+𝑏1√𝑑)𝑁(𝑎2+𝑏2√𝑑)=𝑁(𝑎1+𝑏1√𝑑𝑎2+𝑏2√𝑑)N(a1+b1d)N(a2+b2d)=N(a1+b1da2+b2d)

    Additionally, the reciprocal of a quadratic algebraic number is the ratio of its conjugate to its norm:

    1𝑎+𝑏√𝑑=𝑎−𝑏√𝑑𝑁(𝑎+𝑏√𝑑)1a+bd=a−bdN(a+bd)

    Using Vieta's theorem, the quadratic algebraic number 𝛼α is actually a root of the equation

    𝑥2−tr⁡(𝛼)𝑥+𝑁(𝛼)=0x2−tr⁡(α)x+N(α)=0

    The discriminant of this equation is also called the discriminant of the quadratic algebraic number, denoted disc⁡(𝛼)disc⁡(α), with value 4𝑑𝑏24db2.

    Matrix representation

    Similar to complex numbers, quadratic algebraic numbers can also be represented by matrices. Let 𝑑 ≠0,1d≠0,1 be a squarefree integer and 𝑎,𝑏a,b be rational numbers, then 𝑎 +𝑏√𝑑a+bd can be represented by the matrix

    (𝑎𝑏𝑑𝑏𝑎)(abdba)

    It can be verified that the results of addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division of this matrix correspond to the operations on quadratic algebraic numbers. The trace and determinant of this matrix correspond to the trace and norm of the quadratic algebraic number, and the discriminant of the characteristic polynomial of the matrix is the discriminant of the quadratic algebraic number. The adjugate matrix of this matrix corresponds to the conjugate of the quadratic algebraic number.

    Quadratic Integer Rings

    Among quadratic algebraic numbers, the more special ones are quadratic integers. A quadratic integer is a complex number that can be expressed as a solution of a univariate quadratic equation with leading coefficient one and integer coefficients. The only difference from quadratic algebraic numbers is the restriction on the leading coefficient. By the quadratic formula, the two roots of the quadratic equation 𝑥2 +𝑝𝑥 +𝑞 =0x2+px+q=0 are

    −𝑝±√𝑝2−4𝑞2−p±p2−4q2

    If 𝑝p is even, i.e., 𝑝 =2𝑘p=2k, then the two roots can be written as −𝑘 ±√𝑘2−𝑞−k±k2−q; if 𝑝p is odd, i.e., 𝑝 =2𝑘 +1p=2k+1, then the two roots can be written as −𝑘 −1±√4(𝑘2+𝑘−𝑞)+12−k−1±4(k2+k−q)+12.     𝑎+𝑏𝜔a+bω

    where 𝑎a and 𝑏b are integers, and

    𝜔=⎧{ {⎨{ {⎩1+√𝑑2,𝑑≡1(mod4),√𝑑,𝑑≡2,3(mod4).ω={1+d2,d≡1(mod4),d,d≡2,3(mod4).

    Conversely, any complex number of this form is a quadratic integer. The representation of each non-rational quadratic integer in this form is unique.

    The set of all quadratic integers in the quadratic field 𝐐(√𝑑)Q(d) is denoted 𝐙[𝜔]Z[ω]. Since this set is closed under addition, subtraction, and multiplication, it is also called a quadratic integer ring. The rational numbers in the quadratic integer ring are exactly all integers. If we consider the set of all ratios of quadratic integers in 𝐙[𝜔]Z[ω], we obtain the corresponding quadratic field 𝐐(√𝑑)Q(d).

    The trace, norm, and discriminant of quadratic integers are all integers. The minimum value of the discriminants of all quadratic irrationals in the quadratic integer ring 𝐙[𝜔]Z[ω] is also called the discriminant of the corresponding quadratic field 𝐐(√𝑑)Q(d). When 𝑑 ≡1(mod4)d≡1(mod4), the discriminant is 𝑑d; when 𝑑 ≡2,3(mod4)d≡2,3(mod4), the discriminant is 4𝑑4d.

    Divisibility, Associates, and Units

    Similar to integers, a theory of divisibility can be established for quadratic integers. Of course, the discussion must be carried out within the same quadratic integer ring.

    For quadratic integers 𝛼α and 𝛽β in the quadratic integer ring 𝐙[𝜔]Z[ω], if there exists a quadratic integer 𝛾γ in the same ring such that 𝛽 =𝛼𝛾β=αγ holds, we say that 𝛼α divides 𝛽β, denoted 𝛼 ∣𝛽α∣β. Divisibility is a partial order relation on the quadratic integer ring. If simultaneously 𝛼 ∣𝛽α∣β and 𝛽 ∣𝛼β∣α, then 𝛼α and 𝛽β can be regarded as the same number in the study of divisibility theory; they are called associates. Associate relation is an equivalence relation on the quadratic integer ring.

    Analogous to the divisibility theory of integers, the associate relation for integers is being negatives of each other. Divisibility theory usually only needs to consider natural numbers, not negative numbers. For quadratic integers, the associate relation may be somewhat more complex. If quadratic integers 𝛼α and 𝛽β are associates, then there exist quadratic integers 𝛾γ and 𝛿δ such that 𝛽 =𝛼𝛾β=αγ and 𝛼 =𝛽𝛿α=βδ hold. Therefore, the ratio of 𝛽β and 𝛼α, namely 𝛾γ, must be a special quadratic integer, i.e., there exists a quadratic integer 𝛿δ such that 𝛾𝛿 =1γδ=1. Such a quadratic integer 𝛾γ is called a unit, also called a reversible element. Two quadratic integers are associates if and only if their ratio is a unit. Therefore, to understand the associate relation on the quadratic integer ring, one must understand the structure of its units.

    Since the norm can preserve multiplication, and the norm of a quadratic integer is always an integer, using the norm can transform the divisibility relation of quadratic integers into the divisibility relation of integers. That is, if 𝛼 ∣𝛽α∣β, then necessarily 𝑁(𝛼) ∣𝑁(𝛽)N(α)∣N(β). Similarly, a quadratic integer 𝛼α is a unit if and only if its norm 𝑁(𝛼) = ±1N(α)=±1. Therefore, to obtain all units in the quadratic integer ring 𝐙[𝜔]Z[ω], one only needs to solve the Diophantine equation:

    𝑁(𝑎+𝑏√𝑑)=1N(a+bd)=1

    where the expression for the norm is

    𝑁(𝑎+𝑏√𝑑)=⎧{ {⎨{ {⎩𝑎2+𝑎𝑏+1−𝑑4𝑏2,𝑑≡1(mod4),𝑎2−𝑑𝑏2,𝑑≡2,3(mod4).N(a+bd)={a2+ab+1−d4b2,d≡1(mod4),a2−db2,d≡2,3(mod4).

    For imaginary quadratic integer rings, i.e., when 𝑑 <0d<0, since the norm is necessarily a nonnegative integer, it can be easily verified that for all squarefree negative numbers 𝑑 ≠ −1, −3d≠−1,−3, the only solutions are (𝑎,𝑏) =( ±1,0)(a,b)=(±1,0). That is, except for 𝐙[√−1]Z[−1] and 𝐙[1+√−32]Z[1+−32], the units in imaginary quadratic integer rings are only ±1±1. Let i =√−1i=−1, then the quadratic integer ring 𝐙[i]Z[i] is also called the Gaussian integer ring, and its set of units is { ±1, ±i}{±1,±i}. Let 𝜔 =1+√−32ω=1+−32, then the quadratic integer ring 𝐙[𝜔]Z[ω] is also called the Eisenstein integer ring, and its set of units is { ±1, ±𝜔, ±𝜔2}{±1,±ω,±ω2}.

    For real quadratic integer rings, i.e., when 𝑑 >0d>0, the situation is more complex and can be transformed into a discussion of the corresponding Pell equation. From the related conclusions about the Pell equation, it can be known that the set of all units in this case can be written as { ±𝑢𝑘 :𝑘 ∈𝐙}{±uk:k∈Z}, where 𝑢u is called the fundamental unit. The fundamental unit can be solved through the corresponding Pell equation. The fundamental unit is not unique: if 𝑢u is a fundamental unit, then its conjugate ¯𝑢u¯ as well as −𝑢−u and −¯𝑢−u¯ are all fundamental units.

    The structure of units in quadratic integer rings can be generalized to general algebraic integer rings. The Dirichlet unit theorem in algebraic number theory states that all units in any algebraic integer ring form a finitely generated abelian group, and this theorem also gives the rank of this group.

    The content in the divisibility theory of integers, such as the greatest common divisor, Euclidean division, Bezout's theorem, and the unique factorization theorem, can all be generalized to some or all quadratic integer rings. Whether such a generalization can be carried out on a certain quadratic integer ring actually reflects whether the quadratic integer ring is sufficiently similar to the integer ring. The unique factorization theorem does not hold on all quadratic integer rings; even among quadratic integer rings where the unique factorization theorem holds, only some of them allow Euclidean division.     Unique Factorization

    If the unique factorization theorem of integers can be generalized to a quadratic integer ring, it should have the following form: every quadratic integer in the ring 𝐙[𝜔]Z[ω] can be written as a product of irreducible elements, and this decomposition is unique up to associate and order. An irreducible element is a quadratic integer that cannot be further decomposed into a product of non-units, similar to prime numbers in the unique factorization theorem of integers. As mentioned earlier, not all quadratic integer rings satisfy the unique factorization theorem.

    For example, in 𝐙[√−5]Z[−5], there is the decomposition 9 =3 ×3 =(2 +√−5) ×(2 −√−5)9=3×3=(2+−5)×(2−−5), but 33 and 2 ±√−52±−5 are already irreducible elements, so the decomposition is not unique. To show that these three numbers are all irreducible, one can examine their norms: their norms are all 99, and if they can be decomposed into a product of non-units, then the norm of the factor in the decomposition can only be 33, but there is no quadratic integer with norm 33 in 𝐙[√−5]Z[−5].

    The main reason that the unique factorization theorem does not hold in general quadratic integer rings is that the decomposition using only quadratic integers is not thorough enough. For example, to decompose the product 𝑎𝑏𝑐𝑑abcd, but the available basic elements are only {𝑎𝑏,𝑐𝑑,𝑎𝑐,𝑏𝑑}{ab,cd,ac,bd}, then the obtained decomposition is naturally not unique; to obtain the unique factorization theorem, one must further consider {𝑎,𝑏,𝑐,𝑑}{a,b,c,d}. In quadratic integer rings, the structure finer than quadratic integers is the ideals of the quadratic integer ring. Mapping a quadratic integer to its principal ideal can embed its associate equivalence class into the set of ideals, so the decomposition of integers is just a special case of the decomposition of ideals. If we consider the decomposition of ideals in quadratic integer rings, it can be proved that all ideals in quadratic integer rings can be uniquely decomposed into a product of prime ideals. This shows that all quadratic integer rings are Dedekind domains. More generally, all algebraic integer rings are Dedekind domains.

    If the unique factorization theorem holds on a quadratic integer ring, then its prime ideals correspond one-to-one with irreducible elements (up to associate), therefore, for this type of quadratic integer ring, decomposing ideals into prime ideals is equivalent to decomposing quadratic integers into irreducible elements. Moreover, the irreducible elements in this case are also called prime elements, which correspond to the concept of prime numbers in integers. The following discussion will use prime ideals and related concepts; readers unfamiliar with this concept can replace them word-for-word with prime elements, and these statements will also be correct in cases where the unique factorization theorem holds.
