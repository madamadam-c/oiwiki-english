    Polynomials and Generating Functions Overview

    Polynomials and Generating Functions

    Manipulating finite/infinite polynomials is an important content in OI mathematics, especially in generating functions.

    Polynomial algorithms based on Fast Fourier Transform give algorithm competition players the ability to directly manipulate generating functions.

    Basic Concepts

    For the summation ∑𝑎𝑛𝑥𝑛∑anxn, if it is a finite sum, it is called a polynomial, denoted as 𝑓(𝑥) =∑𝑚𝑛=0𝑎𝑛𝑥𝑛f(x)=∑n=0manxn.

    A summable sum is called a series. In the sum ∑∞𝑛=0𝑎𝑛𝑥𝑛∑n=0∞anxn, each term is a constant multiplied by a non-negative integer power function—this form is called a power series.

    When studying polynomial arithmetic, we first consider simpler polynomials; the concept of power series is only used for convenience in understanding. In mathematical analysis, the convergence of power series will be further studied.

    For the general definitions of rings, fields, and their derived structures, see Basic Concepts of Abstract Algebra.

    For a general ring 𝑅R, define the polynomial ring 𝑅[𝑥]R[x] over 𝑅R.

    Each element 𝑓f is called a polynomial over 𝑅R, which can be expressed as:

    𝑓=⟨𝑓0,𝑓1,𝑓2,⋯,𝑓𝑛⟩(𝑓0,𝑓1,𝑓2,⋯,𝑓𝑛∈𝑅)f=⟨f0,f1,f2,⋯,fn⟩(f0,f1,f2,⋯,fn∈R)

    In other words, we directly define a polynomial as a sequence of coefficients. It can also be expressed as:

    𝑓(𝑥)=𝑓0+𝑓1𝑥+𝑓2𝑥2+⋯+𝑓𝑛𝑥𝑛f(x)=f0+f1x+f2x2+⋯+fnxn

    Here we consider 𝑥x only as a formal symbol, a marker for coefficient positions.

    If we also allow infinitely many terms:

    𝑓(𝑥)=𝑓0+𝑓1𝑥+𝑓2𝑥2+⋯f(x)=f0+f1x+f2x2+⋯

    Then we get the formal power series ring 𝑅[[𝑥]]R[[x]]. Each element 𝑓f in it is called a formal power series, abbreviated as power series.

    Degree of a Polynomial

    For a polynomial 𝑓(𝑥)f(x), the degree of its highest-order term is called its degree, denoted as deg⁡𝑓deg⁡f.

    Polynomial Multiplication

    The core operation is the multiplication of two polynomials, i.e., given polynomials 𝑓(𝑥)f(x) and 𝑔(𝑥)g(x):

    𝑓(𝑥)=𝑎0+𝑎1𝑥+⋯+𝑎𝑛𝑥𝑛(1)𝑔(𝑥)=𝑏0+𝑏1𝑥+⋯+𝑏𝑚𝑥𝑚(2)f(x)=a0+a1x+⋯+anxn(1)g(x)=b0+b1x+⋯+bmxm(2)

    To compute the polynomial 𝑄(𝑥) =𝑓(𝑥) ⋅𝑔(𝑥)Q(x)=f(x)⋅g(x):

    𝑄(𝑥)=𝑛∑𝑖=0𝑚∑𝑗=0𝑎𝑖𝑏𝑗𝑥𝑖+𝑗=𝑐0+𝑐1𝑥+⋯+𝑐𝑛+𝑚𝑥𝑛+𝑚Q(x)=∑i=0n∑j=0maibjxi+j=c0+c1x+⋯+cn+mxn+m

    Polynomial or power series multiplication satisfies associativity and distributivity over addition. If 𝑅R is a commutative ring or unity ring, multiplication correspondingly satisfies commutativity and has an identity element.

    If there exist 2𝑛2n-th roots of unity on 𝑅R, Fast Fourier Transform allows us to compute the product of two degree-2𝑛2n polynomials in 𝑂(𝑛2𝑛)O(n2n) instead of 𝑂(22𝑛)O(22n) time.

    (+∞∑𝑘=0𝑓𝑘𝑥𝑘)′=+∞∑𝑘=1𝑘𝑓𝑘𝑥𝑘−1(∑k=0+∞fkxk)′=∑k=1+∞kfkxk−1

    𝑘𝑓𝑘=𝑓𝑘+𝑓𝑘+⋯+𝑓𝑘⏟__⏟__⏟𝑘 copies of 𝑓𝑘kfk=fk+fk+⋯+fk⏟k copies of fk

    The basic differentiation rules—sum rule, product rule, and chain rule (when composition is allowed)—remain correct.

    If division is allowed on 𝑅R, we can similarly define the formal indefinite integral of a formal power series.

    Multiplicative Inverse

    From the example:

    11−𝑥=1+𝑥+𝑥2+⋯11−x=1+x+x2+⋯

    We can see that the reciprocal of a polynomial can be expanded into an infinite series. The reciprocal exists if and only if the constant term is non-zero, and the reciprocal also has a non-zero constant term.

    Therefore, for a formal power series 𝑓f, if 𝑓0 ≠0f0≠0, its multiplicative inverse 𝑓−1f−1 is another formal power series satisfying:

    𝑓×𝑓−1=𝑓−1×𝑓=1f×f−1=f−1×f=1

    𝑓−10=1𝑓0,𝑓−1𝑛=−1𝑓0𝑛−1∑𝑘=0𝑓−1𝑘𝑓𝑛−𝑘f0−1=1f0,fn−1=−1f0∑k=0n−1fk−1fn−k

    Computing the first 𝑛n terms directly using the recurrence formula is 𝑂(𝑛2)O(n2). Using FFT, we can get an 𝑂(𝑛log⁡𝑛)O(nlog⁡n) algorithm. See Polynomial Inverse.

    Note

    It is easy to find that the reciprocal of 𝑓(𝑥)f(x) is the infinite-term Maclaurin expansion (infinite-term Taylor expansion at 𝑥 =0x=0) of 1𝑓(𝑥)1f(x).

    Composition Inverse

    Modular Polynomials

    A modular polynomial is a subring of the polynomial ring, obtained by dividing the polynomial ring by the equivalence relation of congruence.

    In the division with remainder mentioned above, polynomial 𝑓(𝑥)f(x) and its remainder 𝑅(𝑥)R(x) are congruent modulo the polynomial 𝑔(𝑥)g(x):

    𝑓(𝑥)≡𝑅(𝑥)(mod𝑔(𝑥))f(x)≡R(x)(modg(x))

    𝑓(𝑥0)=𝑅(𝑥0)f(x0)=R(x0)

    And if the root 𝑥0x0 has multiplicity 𝑘k in polynomial 𝑔(𝑥)g(x), i.e., (𝑥 −𝑥0)𝑘(x−x0)k divides 𝑔(𝑥)g(x), then for any integer 𝑡t where 0 ≤𝑡 <𝑘0≤t<k:

    𝑓𝑡(𝑥0)=𝑅𝑡(𝑥0)ft(x0)=Rt(x0)

    Here the notation denotes the 𝑡t-th derivative.

    Modular polynomial congruence can be applied to power series. An infinite-term power series can be congruent to a finite-term polynomial under a specific polynomial modulus. For example:

    1+𝑥+𝑥2+𝑥3+…≡1+𝑥+…+𝑥𝑛−1(mod𝑥𝑛)1+x+x2+x3+…≡1+x+…+xn−1(modxn)

    In some specific cases, we can also modulo other polynomials; the corresponding situations will be explained below.

    Multi-Point Evaluation and Interpolation of Polynomials

    Multi-point evaluation of polynomials gives a polynomial 𝑓(𝑥)f(x) and 𝑛n points 𝑥1,𝑥2,…,𝑥𝑛x1,x2,…,xn, and asks for:

    𝑓(𝑥1),𝑓(𝑥2),…,𝑓(𝑥𝑛)f(x1),f(x2),…,f(xn)

    Polynomial interpolation gives 𝑛 +1n+1 points:

    (𝑥0,𝑦0),(𝑥1,𝑦1),…,(𝑥𝑛,𝑦𝑛)(x0,y0),(x1,y1),…,(xn,yn)

    Find an 𝑛n-degree polynomial 𝑓(𝑥)f(x) such that all 𝑛 +1n+1 points lie on 𝑓(𝑥)f(x).

    The essence of these two operations is converting between coefficient representation and point-value representation of polynomials. Multi-point evaluation converts coefficient representation to point-value representation, and interpolation converts point-value representation to coefficient representation.

    Note

    From the perspective of power series, multi-point evaluation "compresses" infinite-term information into finitely many point values, thus losing some information; interpolation restores to the corresponding degree coefficient representation.

    Common programming evaluation and interpolation, such as discrete Fourier transform (and its inverse), choose 𝑛 +1n+1 points with multiplicity 1 (i.e., all different), avoiding the trouble of derivatives.

    This "compression" only guarantees consistency at the 𝑛 +1n+1 points. From the explanation of modular polynomial congruence above, if the power series 𝑓(𝑥)f(x) is evaluated at 𝑥0x0 to 𝑥𝑛xn and then interpolated to get polynomial 𝑅(𝑥)R(x), then letting:

    𝑔(𝑥)=(𝑥−𝑥0)…(𝑥−𝑥𝑛)g(x)=(x−x0)…(x−xn)

    We have:

    𝑓(𝑥)≡𝑅(𝑥)(mod𝑔(𝑥))f(x)≡R(x)(modg(x))

    Since the degree of 𝑅(𝑥)R(x) is strictly less than 𝑔(𝑥)g(x), the 𝑅(𝑥)R(x) obtained through evaluation and interpolation is the remainder. Therefore, in this case, if the power series can be evaluated at the roots, it can be modulo a polynomial. A counterexample is:

    11−𝑥=1+𝑥+𝑥2+𝑥3+…11−x=1+x+x2+x3+…

    It cannot be evaluated at 𝑥 =1x=1, so the series 1 +𝑥 +𝑥2 +𝑥3 +…1+x+x2+x3+… cannot be modulo the polynomial 𝑥 −1x−1.

    Since any-order derivative of a power series always has a value at 00, modulo 𝑥𝑛xn can always be computed, consistent with the meaning of "truncation" above. Discrete Fourier transform (and its inverse) is equivalent to modulo the polynomial 𝑥𝑛 −1xn−1.

    Under the modulus of polynomial ℎ(𝑥)h(x), the power series 𝑓(𝑥)f(x) sometimes has an inverse. The inverse is the remainder obtained by taking the reciprocal of power series 𝑓(𝑥)f(x) modulo polynomial ℎ(𝑥)h(x).

    Generating Functions

    A generating function is a formal power series whose coefficients can provide information about the sequence.

    There are many different types of generating functions, but they can generally be expressed as a single form:
